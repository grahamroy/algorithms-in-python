Every article in this track so far has judged one sentence at a time. Is it likely, is it well formed, what comes next. This one takes two sentences and asks what the first tells you about the second:

premise:    the ancient throne commands the banner
hypothesis: the throne does not command the banner
label:      contradiction

Three answers are allowed — the hypothesis follows from the premise, contradicts it, or neither, which is called neutral. That is natural language inference, and it is the task that made the sentence pair the unit of work.

It is also the task that taught the field to distrust its own benchmarks, and that is where this article spends most of its time.

The micro-language from Part 1 gets a semantics so the labels can be computed rather than collected. A sentence now asserts a polarised relation plus whatever adjectives it carries:

the noble king rules his crown
  -> rule(king, crown) and noble(king)
the king does not rule his crown
  -> NOT rule(king, crown)

Entailment is set inclusion over those facts; contradiction is the same relation with the polarity flipped; everything else is neutral. Negation is new — does not and do not — and it is the only new machinery the stage needs.

A worked example: a function, two readers, and a tell

The label is a function, not an opinion

Because the label is computed, the task can be taken apart and each piece priced. Here is what you score with only part of the rule, on 2,000 held-out pairs:

![[RULE TABLE IMAGE]]

Relation matching — find the premise clause about the same subject, verb and object, then compare polarity — gets 94.90%. The remaining five points are the adjective check: whether the hypothesis asserted something the premise never did. Guessing the majority class gets 34.35%.

Keep the third row in mind. Hypothesis only scores 32.95%, which is chance. That is not an accident; it is a property the generator was built to have, and the rest of this article is about what happens when a dataset does not have it.

Two ways to read a pair

There are two ways to put a pair through a transformer, and the choice turns out to matter more than anything else here.

A cross-encoder reads them joined together — [CLS] premise [SEP] hypothesis [SEP] — in one pass, so any hypothesis token may attend to any premise token. A third embedding table, alongside tokens and positions, marks which side each token is on. This is BERT’s recipe.

A bi-encoder encodes each sentence alone, pools each to a vector, and compares the two with [u; v; |u-v|; u*v]. The two sentences never share a forward pass. This is InferSent’s recipe, and SBERT’s after it.

Same trunk, same 8,000 labels, same budget:

![[READERS TABLE IMAGE]]

The cross-encoder trained from scratch is the interesting failure. It scores 65.30%, and the ceiling for a model that gets entailment and contradiction right while abandoning neutral entirely is 66.7%. Its neutral recall is 9.6%. It found the cheap half of the task — is there a negation mismatch — and stopped looking.

That is the same shortcut-taking this article is about, appearing one level down. The model is not lazy; it is doing what the gradient pays for.

Now read the two pre-training rows. Pre-training is worth +30.0 points to the cross-encoder and +0.9 to the bi-encoder. That asymmetry is the whole result. The bi-encoder’s comparison is wired into its architecture — the subtraction in |u-v| is not learned, it is soldered on, so there is almost nothing for pre-training to add. The cross-encoder’s comparison has to be discovered inside the attention pattern, and 8,000 labels are nowhere near enough to discover it.

What pre-training installs can be measured on its own. Blank a token in the hypothesis and ask the encoder to recover it, once with the premise visible and once with it hidden:

![[ALIGNMENT IMAGE]]

The premise is worth 2.2156 nats. That gap is cross-attention doing its job, and it is the one thing a bi-encoder can never have, because its two sentences never meet.

An honesty note, because the ordering here is not the published one. In real NLI leaderboards cross-encoders beat bi-encoders; in this article the bi-encoder still wins, 98.50% to 95.30%. Both facts point the same way. Joint attention is a capability you pay for, the bill is paid in pre-training, and a budget that fits in a twelve-minute script buys most of the gap but not the crossover. The measured +30.0 is the shape of that bill.

The artefact

SNLI, the dataset that started modern NLI, was built by showing annotators a photo caption and asking for three sentences: one that must be true, one that must be false, and one that might be true. Hundreds of thousands of times.

People took the shortest path. For must be false they negated. For must be true they deleted a detail. For might be true they added one. Nobody was being careless on purpose; it is simply what the task rewards at speed.

So this article ships two generators. The careless one does exactly that. The balanced one puts a negation in half the hypotheses of every class and an adjective in half of them, and matches the lengths:

![[ARTEFACT TABLE IMAGE]]

Look at the careless block. Negation appears in 100% of contradictions and 0% of everything else. An adjective appears in 100% of neutrals and nowhere else. Mean hypothesis length runs 5.0, 6.0, 7.0 — one word per class. The label is written on the hypothesis three separate ways.

Now train a model that is shown only the hypothesis. The premise is not summarised or truncated; it is removed:

![[TRANSFER TABLE IMAGE]]

100.00% on a three-way entailment task, from a model that has never seen a premise. It has learned nothing whatsoever about entailment. It has learned how the data was made.

Then move that same model to the balanced data and it falls to 33.00%, against a chance rate of 33.3%. Everything it knew was the tell.

This is not a hypothetical. Gururangan et al. and Poliak et al. independently found in 2018 that a hypothesis-only model gets around 67% on SNLI — against 34% chance — and the leaderboard had been running for three years by then. Every number published on that benchmark included an unknown amount of this.

It is also why the gold labels in this article are computed by a function. A generator can be audited line by line. A pool of annotators cannot.

The label as a token

Part 5’s decoder answered questions by predicting the next word. Nothing stops it answering this one the same way: read the pair, then predict yes, no or maybe.

[CLS] the ancient throne commands the banner [SEP]
the throne does not command the banner [SEP] no

No classification head anywhere — the answer comes out of the same vocabulary the text did. Given the same trunk, the same pre-training budget and the same labels:

![[DECODER TABLE IMAGE]]

The decoder reaches 66.45% against the cross-encoder’s 95.30%, and almost all of the difference is neutral, where its recall is 24.0%. The answer’s format is not the problem: the position that predicts the label sits at the very end and can see the whole pair.

What it lacks is the other direction. In a bidirectional encoder, every premise token is re-read once the hypothesis arrives — throne in the premise gets to notice that throne appears again on the other side of the [SEP]. Causality forbids exactly that. The premise is encoded before the hypothesis exists, and comparison is a two-way operation.

That is Part 4’s lesson arriving somewhere new. There, the evidence that a causal model could not reach lay to the right within one sentence. Here it lies in the other sentence, and it is the premise, not the hypothesis, that needed to see ahead.

Big-O and complexity

![[BIG-O TABLE IMAGE]]

The row that decides architectures in practice is the last one. A bi-encoder’s sentence vectors do not depend on what they are compared against, so they can be computed once and cached; scoring a new hypothesis against N stored premises is N dot products. A cross-encoder has to run the whole transformer N times, because its representation of the premise depends on the hypothesis sitting next to it.

For a leaderboard with 10,000 test pairs that difference is an inconvenience. For search over ten million documents it is the difference between a product and a research demo — which is why the retrieval half of the next few articles is built on bi-encoders, and why anything that needs real accuracy re-ranks the top fifty with a cross-encoder afterwards.

Where NLI sits

NLI is old — it was the Recognising Textual Entailment challenge in 2005, with a few thousand hand-built pairs. SNLI (Bowman et al., 2015) made it a deep-learning task by making it large: 570,000 pairs, enough to train on. MultiNLI added genres. For about three years it was the benchmark the field steered by, and GLUE put it at the centre.

Then came the artefact papers, and the lesson generalised well beyond NLI: a benchmark measures the dataset as much as the model, and the only way to know which is to run the ablation that removes the input. Hypothesis-only here; image-only in visual question answering; question-only in reading comprehension. The ablation is cheap and almost nobody ran it for three years.

The lineage in this series is direct. Part 4 built the encoder and showed what pre-training buys a single sentence; this article shows the same purchase being made for a pair, and the price tag is larger. Part 5 built the decoder and scored sentences with no head at all; this article asks it a three-way question and finds the one shape of evidence its mask forbids.

The complete code

The full script is on GitHub — grab it and run it:

nli.py

Run it with:

pip install numpy
python nli.py

It needs only numpy. It runs in about twelve minutes, which is the longest script in this series, because pre-training is the thing being measured and there is no way to measure it quickly — set FAST = True at the top of the file for a three-minute run with the same shape and weaker numbers. Everything is from scratch: segment embeddings, the cross-encoder, the bi-encoder with its [u; v; |u-v|; u*v] comparison, the hypothesis-only ablation, masked and next-token pre-training, and the label-as-a-token decoder — all hand-differentiated and audited against central differences at startup (worst error 7.78e-10 over 372 sampled gradients, with the semantics checked against both generators on all 20,000 pairs). The headline insight worth pinning to the wall: a model shown only the hypothesis and never the premise scores 100.00% on three-way entailment when the data is built the way SNLI was built, and 33.00% — chance — once the negations and lengths are balanced across classes, which means a benchmark number is a statement about a dataset before it is a statement about a model; and the architecture that can in principle read both sentences together is the one that cannot do it without help, because pre-training is worth +30.0 points to a cross-encoder and +0.9 to a bi-encoder whose comparison is soldered in rather than learned.
