# Word Embeddings — You Shall Know a Word by the Company It Keeps

### *Algorithms in Python --- Natural Language Processing, Part 1*

---

Section 12 closed with four ways to manufacture supervision out
of unlabelled data. This section takes that idea to its most
consequential habitat — language — and begins where the modern
era began: with the discovery that a humble pretext on raw text
packs meaning into vectors so well that *arithmetic does
analogy*. Word2vec (Mikolov et al., 2013), GloVe (Pennington et
al., 2014), fastText (Bojanowski et al., 2017) — one shared
idea:

```
    predict the word next door
```

Skip-gram with negative sampling (SGNS) trains each word's
vector to score its actual neighbours high and five randomly
sampled impostors low. Nothing else. No labels, no grammar, no
dictionary — Section 12's self-supervision, five years earlier
and one modality over.

The stage is new, and deliberately artificial: a **synthetic
micro-language**, so that every famous claim about embeddings
can be scored against ground truth instead of folklore. Three
topics (royal, farm, sea); gendered noun pairs whose gender is
carried — as in English — by agreement (*his/her*); plural
morphology carried by verb agreement (*rules/rule*) and
*their*; noun-specific role signatures; and three plural forms
held out of the corpus entirely for the out-of-vocabulary test.
106 words, 165,166 tokens, generated by seeded NumPy:

```
    the tides navigate their captain
    the tractor grazes the fields
    the distant gull sails the sailor
```

We know exactly what the geometry *should* contain — so we can
measure whether it does. The SGNS pair gradients are
hand-derived and audited at startup (1.94e-10 against central
differences).

## A worked example: the map, the parallelogram, the receipt, the pieces

### Predict the word next door, get a map

```
    king    : prince, kings, lord, queens, queen
    queen   : queens, sword, princess, lady, castle
    cow     : cows, hen, barn, bulls, bull
    ship    : ships, storm, wave, mast, tide
    sails   : navigates, drifts, anchors, drift, navigate
    kings   : queens, princes, castles, thrones, swords

    mean cosine: gender partners 0.80  >  same topic 0.76
                 >  cross topic 0.23
```

Every neighbour list is right: royalty with royalty, verbs with
their verb family, plurals with plurals. And the constructed
similarity ordering — gender partners closer than topic-mates,
topic-mates closer than strangers — comes back in the cosines.
Nobody told the model about topics, gender or number; it was
told to guess neighbours, and the structure of *use* did the
rest. Firth's 1957 dictum — you shall know a word by the
company it keeps — now with coordinates.

### The parallelogram

The party trick that made word2vec famous: `vec(b) − vec(a) +
vec(c)`, nearest word wins. On our stage the right answers are
known by construction, so it can be *scored* over every
quadruple rather than eyeballed:

```
    king:queen :: prince:?  ->  princess  (ok)
    king:queen :: lord:?    ->  lady      (ok)
    king:queen :: bull:?    ->  cow       (ok)
    king:queen :: rooster:? ->  hen       (ok)

    gender analogies : 100.0%
    plural analogies :  87.5%
```

The gender direction *is* the his/her difference, learned from
agreement alone — king and queen appear in otherwise identical
sentences, so their vector difference isolates exactly what the
corpus treats differently about them. Likewise number, from
verb agreement and *their*. Directions mean things because the
corpus uses them consistently — which is also the cautionary
tale. Any regularity in the text, wanted or not, becomes
geometry: at web scale, `king − man + woman = queen` and
`doctor − man + woman = nurse` were discovered in the same
matrix, and debiasing word embeddings became a research field
of its own.

### The theory receipt, and a tale of two matrices

Levy & Goldberg (2014) proved something lovely: SGNS is not
just *like* matrix factorisation, it *is* matrix factorisation
— implicitly factorising the pointwise mutual information
matrix, shifted by `−log k` (k = the number of negative
samples). On this corpus, over every word pair seen more than
three times:

```
    corr( PMI , win·wout ) : r = 0.90
    slope                  : 0.94   (theory: 1)
    intercept              : -1.76  (theory: -log 5 = -1.61)
```

A theorem checked to two decimals in NumPy. The neural pretext
and the count-based factorisation are the same computation —
which is why GloVe, which factorises the co-occurrence counts
*explicitly* by weighted least squares, belongs in the same
article. Trained here and scored both ways:

```
    GloVe, input matrix W       : gender  75.0%  plural  80.4%
    GloVe, customary sum W + Wt : gender  25.0%  plural  25.0%
    (sum's neighbours of 'king': knight, commands, defends, rules)
```

The input matrix works. The customary `W + W̃` sum — standard,
recommended-by-the-paper practice at scale — collapses on this
corpus, and look at *how* it fails: king's neighbours become
the words king **co-occurs with**, not words **like** king.
Similarity lives *within* each matrix; co-occurrence lives
*between* them; on a small corpus the second swamps the first.
The fourth folklore-transfer lesson in two sections: measure
before you inherit even a vector sum.

### The subword insurance (fastText)

Word2vec keeps one row per word — so a word it has never seen
is not rare, it is *nonexistent*. fastText rebuilds every word
from character 3–5-grams (plus the whole word); a word's vector
is the mean of its pieces. Same pretext, same corpus:

```
    fastText analogies: gender 100.0%  plural  92.9%

    princesses   -> princess, princes, queens, queen
    roosters     -> rooster, hens, tractor, orchards
    compasses    -> compass, island, captain, storm
```

Those last three words were **held out of the corpus
entirely** — never generated, no row, nothing to look up — and
their n-gram vectors land beside their singulars and families
anyway, because `<princess` and `esses>` were seen in *other*
words. Morphology is the insurance policy: it is how fastText
handled typos, rare words, and the rich morphologies (Finnish,
Turkish) that a one-row-per-word ledger cannot. And the
instinct it embodies — build words from learned pieces — is
exactly where this section goes next.

## Big-O and complexity

![[BIG-O TABLE IMAGE]]

The economics that let word2vec eat Wikipedia on a laptop:
each training pair touches `O(k · d)` numbers — the word, its
neighbour, and k impostors — instead of a `V`-wide softmax;
negative sampling is the whole trick, and it is the same
per-pair bill fastText pays (times a handful of n-grams).
GloVe's bill scales with *distinct* co-occurring pairs, not
corpus length — count once, factorise forever. Lookup after
training is an `O(d)` row read, which is why embeddings became
infrastructure: the first layer of every model in this
section, the similarity engine of every retrieval system.
Section 00's vector indexes exist to make this article's
nearest-neighbour half sublinear at scale.

## Where the vectors went

For half a decade these three were NLP's foundation: pretrained
vectors as the input layer of every tagger, parser and
classifier; similarity search and analogy demos as the public
face of "AI understands language". Their static nature — one
vector per word, however many senses it has — became the next
frontier: *bank* the institution and *bank* of the river share
a row, and resolving that requires context, which is BERT's
story later in this section. But the ideas never left. Every
transformer begins with an embedding matrix trained by the
same gradient flow; retrieval systems from search to RAG are
cosine similarity over learned vectors, industrialised;
fastText's subword instinct became BPE tokenisation (Part 2);
and the Levy–Goldberg equivalence — neural pretext = matrix
factorisation — remains the cleanest bridge between the
count-based linguistics of the twentieth century and the
gradient-based language modelling of the twenty-first. The
recommender embeddings of Section 07 and the two-tower
retrieval it serves are this article's mathematics wearing
production clothes.

## What comes next

Part 2, **Tokenisation** (BPE / WordPiece / SentencePiece),
asks the question fastText half-answered: if words should be
built from pieces, who decides the pieces? Byte-pair encoding
learns them from the corpus by greedy merging — the humble
compression algorithm that quietly decides what every modern
language model can and cannot see.

## The complete code

The full script is on GitHub — grab it and run it:

[**word_embeddings.py**](https://github.com/grahamroy/algorithms-in-python/blob/main/13-natural-language-processing/01-word-embeddings/word_embeddings.py)

Run it with:

```bash
pip install numpy
python word_embeddings.py
```

It needs only `numpy` and runs in about two minutes. Everything
is from scratch: the micro-language and its agreement grammar,
vectorised skip-gram with negative sampling (hand gradients,
audited), the PMI receipt, GloVe with AdaGrad, fastText's
n-gram machinery, and every analogy scored against constructed
ground truth. The headline insight worth pinning to the wall:
**predict the word next door and geometry falls out — neighbour
lists organise by topic, gender and number (100% gender
analogies, 87.5% plural) because the corpus's agreement
patterns become directions; the pretext is secretly matrix
factorisation (dot products track shifted PMI at r = 0.90,
intercept −1.76 vs the theorem's −1.61); the same factorisation
done explicitly (GloVe) works from its input matrix but
collapses under the customary two-matrix sum on a small corpus
— similarity lives within a matrix, co-occurrence between them;
and building words from character pieces (fastText) prices
words that were never in the corpus at all — the insurance
policy that became tokenisation**.
