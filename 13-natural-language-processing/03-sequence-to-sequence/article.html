Part 2 decided which pieces a word should be built from. BPE merged the most frequent pairs, WordPiece merged the most surprising, Unigram pruned from above — three algorithms, one promise: every word decomposes into known fragments. But tokens in a row are not yet a language. A tokeniser says what the pieces are; it says nothing about what happens when you read a sequence of them and need to produce another.

This article asks the next question:

    given an input sequence, produce an output sequence.

That is the Seq2Seq problem (Sutskever et al., 2014). An encoder reads the input and compresses it into a fixed-size vector. A decoder unrolls that vector into output tokens, one at a time. Everything the decoder needs must pass through that single vector — the bottleneck.

Bahdanau et al. (2015) broke the bottleneck with attention: instead of one vector, the decoder peeks at every encoder state and picks what is relevant at each step. The model learns alignment — which input tokens matter for each output token — as a side effect.

The stage is still Part 1’s synthetic micro-language — 107 words, three topics — but with a new task: word-order translation from SVO (subject-verb-object) to SOV (subject-object-verb), mirroring real English-to-Japanese/Korean translation. Short sentences (5–6 words) and long sentences (two clauses joined by “and”, 11–13 words) test the bottleneck: the fixed vector struggles on longer sequences; attention does not.

A worked example: encode, decode, attend

The task: SVO to SOV

The micro-language generates SVO sentences. The translation task moves the verb to the end of each clause:

    the thrones crown their queen
    -> the thrones their queen crown
 
    the mast drifts the ship
    -> the mast the ship drifts
 
    the lord defends his banners
    -> the lord his banners defends

For long sentences — two clauses joined by “and” — each clause transforms independently:

    the castle crowns the king and the king defends
    his knight
    -> the castle the king crowns and the king his
    knight defends

The vocabulary is shared between source and target (107 tokens). Training uses 1,500 short pairs and 750 long pairs; the test set has 400 short and 200 long.

The encoder-decoder architecture

The encoder is an Elman RNN that reads the source sequence left to right. At each step t, it updates a hidden state:

    h_t = tanh(W_x · x_t + W_h · h_{t-1} + b)

where x_t is the embedding of the current token. After reading the entire source, the encoder’s final state h_T contains — in theory — everything the decoder needs.

The decoder is a second RNN, initialised with that final state. At each step it receives the previous output token’s embedding, updates its own hidden state, and predicts the next token through a softmax over the vocabulary:

    s_t = tanh(W_x · y_{t-1} + W_h · s_{t-1} + b)
    P(y_t | y_{<t}, x) = softmax(W_o · s_t + b_o)

During training the decoder sees the correct previous token (teacher forcing); during inference it feeds its own predictions back.

Without attention: the bottleneck

Trained for 30 epochs on all 2,250 pairs (lr = 0.002, Adam, gradient clipping at 5.0), the no-attention model converges — the loss drops from 2.05 to 0.95 — but the numbers tell the story:

    Accuracy  short:   4.5%
    Accuracy  long:    0.0%

Not a typo. The model learns the right format — it produces sequences of the right length with real words — but the content is wrong:

    the noble ladies defend their banner
    gold:  the noble ladies their banner defend
    pred:  the noble kings their knight rule  (WRONG)
 
    the fertile cow grazes her tractor and the islands
    sail their harbour
    gold:  the fertile cow her tractor grazes and the
    islands their harbour sail
    pred:  the deep compass the waves anchors and the
    rooster his seed grazes  (WRONG)

The decoder receives one vector — 48 floating-point numbers — and must reconstruct the entire output sequence from it. For a 5-word sentence, that is roughly 10 numbers per word. For a 13-word sentence, fewer than 4. The information does not fit. The bottleneck is real, and it is the reason Sutskever et al.’s original architecture was eventually replaced.

With attention: the bottleneck breaks

Bahdanau attention gives the decoder a window into the full encoder. At each decoding step, the model computes an attention score for every encoder state, then takes a weighted sum:

    e_j = v^T · tanh(W_a · h_j + U_a · s_{t-1})
    a_j = softmax(e)_j
    c_t = sum_j  a_j · h_j

The decoder’s input becomes the concatenation of the previous token’s embedding and the context vector c_t. Now the decoder can reach back into the encoder at every step — it is no longer bottlenecked.

Same architecture (d_emb = 32, d_hid = 48), same training regime, same data. The loss drops from 1.96 to 0.001:

    Accuracy  short: 100.0%
    Accuracy  long:   99.5%

From 4.5% to 100% on short sequences, from 0.0% to 99.5% on long ones. The only change: attention.

    the distant tides navigate their island
    gold:  the distant tides their island navigate
    pred:  the distant tides their island navigate  (ok)
 
    the fertile cow grazes her tractor and the islands
    sail their harbour
    gold:  the fertile cow her tractor grazes and the
    islands their harbour sail
    pred:  the fertile cow her tractor grazes and the
    islands their harbour sail  (ok)

The attention receipt

The attention weights are readable — they show where the decoder looks when producing each output token:

![[ATTENTION TABLE IMAGE]]

Each row is one decoding step; each column is one encoder position. Read down the columns: distant in the output attends 0.99 to distant in the input. tides attends 0.99 to tides. island attends 0.99 to island.

The interesting row is navigate — the verb in the output, which must move from the middle of the input (position 4) to the end of the output (position 6). The decoder assigns 1.00 to the verb’s encoder position. The model learned the alignment between input and output positions as a side effect of the translation task.

This is the same “receipt” idea from Part 4 of the Deep Learning track (Attention Mechanisms): attention weights are human-readable because they sum to 1 and concentrate where the model finds information. The difference is that here, the receipt spans two sequences — encoder and decoder — rather than self-attending within one.

The bottleneck receipt

                        short     long
                        -----     ----
    no attention        4.5%    0.0%
    attention         100.0%   99.5%

Without attention, everything the decoder needs must pass through one vector. Short sequences survive the compression; long ones do not. Attention lets the decoder reach back into the encoder at every step — the bottleneck breaks, and accuracy on long sequences recovers.

The loss curves tell the same story. Without attention, the loss plateaus at 0.95 — the model cannot compress long sequences into one vector, so it learns a generic output distribution instead. With attention, the loss reaches 0.001 — the model finds the exact mapping.

Big-O and complexity

![[BIG-O TABLE IMAGE]]

The encoder runs one RNN step per source token: O(T · d²) where T is source length and d the hidden dimension (the matrix-vector product at each step is O(d²)). The decoder runs one RNN step per target token: O(T' · d²). Attention adds O(T' · T · d) — at each of T' decoding steps, we score all T encoder states. Total: O((T + T') · d² + T' · T · d).

For comparison, the Transformer (Part 5 of the Deep Learning track) replaces the sequential RNN with parallel self-attention: O(n² · d) where n is the full sequence length. Seq2Seq’s RNN is cheaper per-step but inherently sequential — each hidden state depends on the previous one, so the encoder cannot be parallelised. This is the architectural bottleneck that the Transformer removed, and the reason Seq2Seq is no longer the dominant paradigm. But the encoder-decoder structure survived: T5, BART, and machine translation models still use it.

Where Seq2Seq sits

Seq2Seq (Sutskever et al., 2014) launched neural machine translation. The original paper used LSTMs and reversed the source sentence to help the decoder; Bahdanau et al. (2015) added attention and the field never looked back. Google’s Neural Machine Translation system (Wu et al., 2016) scaled the idea to production, and for three years Seq2Seq with attention was the state of the art for translation, summarisation, and dialogue.

The connection to this series is direct. Part 1 built word embeddings — dense vectors that encode meaning. Part 2 decided which subword pieces to use. This part takes sequences of those pieces and produces new sequences from them. The encoder is the same RNN from Part 3 of the Deep Learning track (RNN / LSTM / GRU); the attention mechanism is Part 4’s; the decoder is the autoregressive generation pattern that GPT will later use without an encoder. Seq2Seq is where NLP became generative — the first architecture that could read a sentence and write a different one.

The two halves became the building blocks of everything that followed. BERT (Part 4, next) is the encoder: it reads bidirectionally and produces contextual representations. GPT (Part 5) is the decoder: it writes left-to-right, one token at a time. The Transformer kept the encoder-decoder split but replaced the RNN with self-attention — and that is the architecture we will reach in two parts.

The complete code

The full script is on GitHub — grab it and run it:

seq2seq.py

Run it with:

pip install numpy
python seq2seq.py

It needs only numpy and runs in about two minutes. Everything is from scratch: the Elman RNN encoder, Bahdanau attention with learned alignment, the decoder with teacher forcing and greedy inference, hand-derived BPTT gradients audited against central differences (worst-case relative error: 1.05e-06), and Adam with gradient clipping. The headline insight worth pinning to the wall: the encoder-decoder bottleneck is real and measurable — 4.5% accuracy on short sequences and 0.0% on long ones when the decoder has only one vector to work with; 100% and 99.5% when attention lets it reach back into the encoder at every step; the attention weights are human-readable (the verb in the output attends 1.00 to the verb in the input, nouns attend 0.99 to their source positions), making the model’s alignment decisions as transparent as a lookup table; this is the architecture that launched neural machine translation and whose two halves — encoder and decoder — became BERT and GPT.
