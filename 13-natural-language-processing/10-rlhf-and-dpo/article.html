Every article in this track so far has trained against a target that was known. The next word. A label a function computed. The object a document named. Even Part 9, taking apart what fine-tuning changed, had an accuracy to point at.

Preference learning has none of that. All it is given is the statement that one output was better than another, and the first thing that has to be built is a loss assembled out of comparisons:

P(a > b) = sigma(r(a) - r(b))

That much is standard, and the rest of the pipeline follows from it. Fit a reward model to the comparisons. Optimise a policy against that model. Ship the policy.

The problem is the last step, and it is not an engineering problem. Once the policy has been optimised against the reward model, the only number anyone can measure is the reward model’s opinion of the result — which is precisely the quantity that was maximised, and therefore the one that carries no information about whether anything improved.

In a real pipeline the true objective lives in people’s heads and the question is unanswerable. Here it is a function.

A worked example: a reward you can check

The thing a real pipeline does not have

The true reward is defined over Part 1’s micro-language — three features the language already carries, weighted so no single one dominates:

r*(s) = 1.0  if the sentence carries an adjective
      + 0.7  if the object is the subject's role partner
      + 0.4  if the subject is a royal noun

![[REWARD TABLE IMAGE]]

Because it is a function of the sentence, and because the language enumerates, every quantity RLHF normally estimates is exact at every step: the reward the policy is actually earning, the reward the model thinks it is earning, the grammatical mass, and the drift from where it started.

The reference policy — Part 7’s decoder, untouched — scores 0.7689. The ceiling is 2.1, reached by 192 of the 31,740 sentences. Everything that follows is an attempt to move the first number towards the second using nothing but comparisons.

Fitting a reward model to noisy comparisons

3,000 comparisons are drawn by sampling pairs from the reference policy and labelling them Bradley-Terry style, so 69.37% of the labels actually point at the better sentence and the rest are the noise any human comparison carries.

![[REWARD MODEL TABLE IMAGE]]

Now the diagnostic that matters, and the one I got wrong before measuring it. The obvious story is that the reward model is accurate where it was trained and inaccurate elsewhere, so optimisation walks off a cliff. Over the whole language its correlation with the truth is 0.6906; weighted by where the reference policy actually puts its mass, 0.7092.

Those are the same number. The reward model is a perfectly reasonable judge of sentences, on and off the reference distribution alike. If it were simply a bad model there would be no puzzle here — and the thing that actually goes wrong would stay hidden.

Which leaves a question the correlation cannot answer, because it is computed over the language, and the language is all the sentences there are:

What does a model trained only on grammatical text think about text that is not?

Goodhart, with a number attached

![[RLHF TABLE IMAGE]]

Read the beta = 0.05 row twice. The true reward climbs from 0.7689 to 1.1985, then ends at 0.3442 — below where it started. The proxy does not come down with it.

Nothing went wrong with the optimiser. It maximised exactly what it was given.

The mass column names the mechanism: grammatical output collapses from 0.9770 to 0.2350. So look directly at what the wrecked policy emits, and at what the reward model makes of it:

grammatical          0.0150
mean r* (truth)      0.0225
mean r^ (proxy)      1.7109
mean r^ on the reference policy   -0.3150

The reward model rates this output two full points above the real text it was trained on. The samples say why:

golden golden king feeds his sailor  r^=1.520  r*=0.0
the ancient king rules his sailor    r^=1.595  r*=0.0

The first is the whole article in one line. The reward model pays 1.0 for an adjective, so the policy supplied two — producing something that is not a sentence in any grammar. The second takes the royal bonus and pairs a king with a sailor, breaking the topic agreement that makes a string a sentence at all.

The reward model learned features. The policy supplied features without the form. And nothing in a loss built out of comparisons between grammatical sentences ever had occasion to say that the form was a precondition — every pair it was ever shown had it.

What the KL penalty is actually for

The standard fix is to penalise drift from the reference policy, folding the KL term into the reward as InstructGPT does. Swept across four strengths, with three seeds each, the result is not the trade-off it is usually described as:

![[BETA CURVE IMAGE]]

At beta = 0.20 the peak is 1.5045 — higher than either looser setting ever reached — and it is still there at step 300, at 1.4953 with grammatical mass 0.9800. The loose penalties do not buy a higher maximum in exchange for instability. They simply destroy themselves: at 0.02 the policy ends at 0.0025 with mass 0.0019, which is to say it ends up emitting essentially nothing valid at all.

So the penalty is not a safety tax paid out of performance. At the right strength it wins on both axes, because a policy that runs off the distribution its reward model understands does not earn a better score for it. It earns a score nobody should believe.

Removing the thing that got hacked

DPO drops the reward model entirely and trains on the comparisons directly, treating the log-ratio to the reference policy as an implicit reward. Same 3,000 pairs, same seeds, same budget — and no sampling anywhere, so there is no distribution for a proxy to drift off.

![[DPO TABLE IMAGE]]

DPO rises and stays up. It never reaches what well-tuned RLHF reaches — 1.2820 against 1.4953 — but it also never produces the 0.0025 that the worst RLHF run ends at.

That is the honest summary. DPO is not better at finding reward. It is better at not losing it, because the failure mode it removes is the one that needs a proxy to exist. What it cannot do is exceed its pairs: every sentence it learns from was written by the reference policy, so it can only re-weight what that policy already said. RLHF samples, which is how it can find sentences nobody showed it — and also how it finds the ones nobody vetted.

Big-O and complexity

![[BIG-O TABLE IMAGE]]

The line worth noticing is that RLHF and DPO share a gradient. Both reduce to

sum_i w_i * grad log pi(s_i)

and differ only in the weights: REINFORCE passes an advantage, DPO passes ±beta(1 - sigma(h)). Everything else is bookkeeping. The expensive asymmetry is that RLHF must sample from the policy every step and score those samples with a second network, while DPO reads from a fixed file.

Where this sits

The comparison-based loss is older than any of this: Bradley and Terry published it in 1952, for ranking sports teams. Christiano et al. brought it to deep reinforcement learning in 2017, and InstructGPT made it the standard recipe for language models in 2022 — reward model, PPO, KL penalty to a frozen reference.

The failure in this article was named early. Amodei et al. listed reward hacking among concrete AI-safety problems in 2016; Gao et al. measured reward-model over-optimisation at scale in 2022 and found the same shape, a true-reward curve that rises and then falls while the proxy keeps climbing. Rafailov et al. published DPO in 2023, showing the RLHF objective has a closed-form optimum that can be trained directly from preferences.

What this article adds is not the phenomenon but the yardstick. The curve Gao et al. had to approximate with a much larger “gold” reward model is, in this universe, arithmetic — which is why the article can say exactly how far below baseline the hacked policy ends up, and show you the sentence that did it.

The lineage in the series is direct. Part 7 built the policy and the exact machinery for measuring a distribution over the language. Part 9’s discipline returns too: every policy figure here is a mean over three seeds, because single runs of a policy-gradient method are not evidence.

What comes next

Part 11, In-Context Learning and Prompting, closes the track with the strangest capability of the lot. Everything in this article changed weights to change behaviour. In-context learning changes behaviour with no gradient step at all — the model reads examples in its prompt and does something it was never trained to do. The question is what mechanism could possibly support that, and whether the micro-language can be made to exhibit it.

The complete code

The full script is on GitHub — grab it and run it:

rlhf.py

Run it with:

pip install numpy
python rlhf.py

It needs only numpy and runs in about sixteen minutes. Everything is from scratch: the Bradley-Terry reward head, the weighted log-probability gradient that REINFORCE and DPO both ride on, the KL-penalised policy update, and the exact evaluation over all 31,740 sentences — hand-differentiated and audited against central differences at startup (worst error 4.51e-09 over 160 sampled gradients). The headline insight worth pinning to the wall: a policy optimised against a learned reward model drove its true reward from 0.7689 up to 1.1985 and then down to 0.3442, below where it started, while the reward model went on reporting success — and the reward model was not even a bad one, scoring 0.6906 against the truth on and off its training distribution alike; what it had never been shown was a non-sentence, so when the policy learned to stack two adjectives and call a king’s servant a sailor, it scored that two full points above the real text it was trained on.
