Part 3 built a machine with two halves. The encoder read a sentence; the decoder wrote a new one; attention was the bridge between them. The article ended by pointing at the halves and naming their descendants: BERT is the encoder, GPT is the decoder.

This one throws the decoder away.

What is left is a stack that reads a sequence and emits a vector per position — and immediately a problem. A decoder trains itself on raw text for free: predict the next token, and every token in the corpus is a labelled example. An encoder has no next token. It has no output to compare against. So what do you train it on?

    hide a word, and predict it from what surrounds it

That is masked language modelling, and it is the whole idea behind BERT (Devlin et al., 2019). It is also Section 12’s masked autoencoder, one modality over: hide part of the input, reconstruct it, and the representations you needed in order to reconstruct turn out to be the ones you wanted all along.

Hiding a word has one consequence a decoder can never have. Once the target is in the middle rather than at the end, the model is free to read forward as well as back — and the causal mask, which Part 3 needed so the decoder could not cheat, becomes pure loss. BERT deletes it. Every position attends to every other, in both directions.

The stage is still Part 1’s synthetic micro-language, and it was built for exactly this measurement. Agreement in that language is carried by the verb form and the possessive, both of which sit to the right of the subject:

    the king   rules  his    crown
    the kings  rule   their  crown

So hide the subject and the evidence for its number and gender lies ahead of the gap, where only a bidirectional reader can find it. Hide the possessive and the evidence lies behind it, where anyone can. One corpus, two slots, opposite verdicts — and no folklore required.

A worked example: hide, predict, transfer

The pretext and its recipe

Every sentence gets a [CLS] token in front and padding to seven positions. Then 15% of the word positions are chosen, and the input is corrupted at them — 80% become [MASK], 10% become a random word, and 10% are left exactly as they were. The model predicts the original token at every chosen position:

    [CLS] the <[MASK]> <[MASK]> their queen
    [CLS] the mast drifts <[MASK]> ship
    [CLS] the <swords> defends his banners
    [CLS] the rooster harvests <[MASK]> seed

Angle brackets mark the chosen positions. The third line shows the random replacement — swords is standing where lord was — and the fourth kind of corruption, the 10% left untouched, is the subtle one: a position may be scored without being visibly disturbed, so the model can never assume that what it reads is what it should keep.

Three encoders are pre-trained for 1,500 steps each: a bidirectional one, a causal one wearing Part 3’s triangular mask, and a bidirectional one whose corruption is 100% [MASK]. All three are 2 layers, 4 heads, d=64 — 114,223 parameters. The masked-LM loss, in nats, against 4.69 for a uniform guess over the 109-token vocabulary:

![[LOSS CURVE IMAGE]]

Those are per-batch losses, which is why they wobble rather than descend politely. The floor is not zero and should not be: a masked subject is genuinely ambiguous — twelve nouns could fill it — so part of that loss is irreducible. The gap between the bidirectional and causal curves is the part that is not.

Keep an eye on the third curve, because it matters later: the 100%-[MASK] encoder reaches the lowest loss of the three, and — as the last section measures — transfers the worst. Pre-training loss is a progress bar, not a target.

What bidirectionality actually buys

Mask one slot at a time in 1,000 held-out sentences and score the prediction against ground truth:

![[RECEIPT TABLE IMAGE]]

Read it by the middle column. Where the evidence lies to the left, the two models are identical — both get the verb’s number and the possessive exactly right, every time. Where the evidence lies to the right, the causal model collapses: 66.7% on the subject’s number, which is precisely the base rate for guessing singular every time, and 20.6% on gender, which is worse than it sounds given that most subjects have no gender to get wrong.

The causal model is not looking in the wrong place. There is no place to look.

the distant [MASK] navigate their island
  gold: tides | bidir: tides | causal: compass
 
the noble [MASK] defend their banner
  gold: ladies | bidir: lords | causal: princess
 
the muddy [MASK] feeds the barns
  gold: plough | bidir: plough | causal: hen

Neither model can be certain which noun was hidden — and the honest row in that table is the fourth one, where even the bidirectional model gets the exact word only 45.5% of the time. It is not guessing blindly: Part 1’s role signatures mean the object is the subject’s signature partner half the time, which is almost exactly the accuracy we measure. What the bidirectional model reliably recovers is the grammar — right number, right gender, right topic, often the wrong noun.

And the receipt is readable, as it has been since Part 4 of the Deep Learning track. Mean attention out of the masked position, averaged over heads:

![[ATTENTION TABLE IMAGE]]

Layer 0 of the bidirectional model spreads its attention evenly over the content words on both sides of the gap; layer 1 concentrates half its weight on their, the token that carries the number. The causal model’s rows are zero everywhere below the mask, because the mask forbids it — so it spends its attention on the adjective behind it and on the gap itself.

Pre-train once, then spend labels

Pre-training is a means, not an end. The end is a task with few labels. Here it is agreement acceptability — a miniature of CoLA, the linguistic-acceptability task in BERT’s own GLUE benchmark. Half the sentences are generated normally; half have their agreement broken:

    ok   the islands drift their storm
    bad  the noble banner defend the lords
    bad  the island navigates their captains
    ok   the wooden fence feeds the cow

Every word is in vocabulary and every sentence has the right shape; only the agreement is wrong. Chance is 50%. Three conditions get the same labels and the same 600 held-out test sentences: the same architecture trained from scratch, the pre-trained encoder with everything frozen except a classifier head on [CLS], and the pre-trained encoder fine-tuned end to end.

![[LABELS TABLE IMAGE]]

At 100 labels, fine-tuning is 24 points ahead of the identical architecture trained from scratch — roughly where from-scratch gets with four times the labels. The pre-trained encoder has never seen a label and never seen an ungrammatical sentence. What it has is representations in which agreement is already encoded, because predicting a masked possessive requires knowing the subject’s number.

The frozen probe is the control, and it is the interesting failure. It never gets past 62%, at any label count. The information is in there — fine-tuning the same weights reaches 95.5% — but it is not linearly readable off the [CLS] vector, because nothing in masked language modelling ever gave [CLS] a job. That is the gap BERT’s second pre-training task, next-sentence prediction, was there to fill. RoBERTa later dropped NSP and leaned on fine-tuning instead, which is the column that works here too. Mean-pooling the token vectors rather than reading [CLS] does measurably better at this scale — 88.2% at 100 labels against 80.0%. [CLS] is a convention, not a mechanism.

The head was the bottleneck

There is a cheaper route, and it is the result I did not expect to be this stark. Score a sentence by masking each position in turn and summing the log probability of the word that was really there — the pseudo-log-likelihood (Salazar et al., 2020). No new parameters at all; the masked-LM head already has an opinion:

![[PLL TABLE IMAGE]]

Twenty labels spent on fitting a single threshold beat twenty labels spent on fine-tuning a classifier head, 90.5% to 54.8%. The knowledge was in the encoder the whole time; the randomly-initialised head was the bottleneck.

Honesty about the second row: the causal model scores these sentences better. Every violation in this task is local and visible from the left — a plural verb after a singular subject is improbable to a left-to-right reader too. That is not a crack in the earlier result, it is the boundary of it. Bidirectionality is not a general superiority; it is an advantage exactly where the evidence lies ahead, which is what the fill-the-gap table measures and this task does not.

Why 80/10/10 and not 100% [MASK]

The usual story about the 10% random replacement is that without it the model would simply trust whatever word it is shown, having only ever been asked about [MASK]. That story is testable. Replace a subject with a noun from a different topic, with no [MASK] anywhere in the sentence, and read the masked-LM head at that position:

![[RECIPE TABLE IMAGE]]

The folklore is wrong, at least here. Neither model echoes the word it was shown. Trained only ever to replace [MASK], the 100%-mask encoder still overrules a real token that does not fit — it just does it less precisely, losing ten points of topic accuracy. Downstream, fine-tuned on the acceptability task, the gap persists but stays modest: 67.2% against 61.0% at 50 labels, 80.0% against 70.8% at 100.

The direction matches BERT’s ablation; the magnitude is a few points on 600 test sentences and is not a result to bet on. The recipe’s published justification rests on ablations over corpora a hundred million times this size. Reproducing the direction is worth something. Reproducing the magnitude would need BERT’s budget.

Big-O and complexity

![[BIG-O TABLE IMAGE]]

Self-attention costs O(n² · d) per layer and the feed-forward block O(n · d²), so an L-layer encoder forward is O(L · n(n·d + d²)) — the same arithmetic as the Transformer in Part 5 of the Deep Learning track, minus the triangular mask. Dropping that mask costs nothing: the scores are computed either way, and the mask only overwrites half of them.

Two rows deserve more than a glance. The first is signal per pass: masked language modelling is trained on 15% of positions, where a causal language model gets a gradient from every token in the sequence. BERT pays for its bidirectionality in sample efficiency, which is why pre-training it takes more passes over more text than training a decoder of the same size.

The second is scoring. A causal model scores a sentence in one forward pass, because its probabilities factorise left to right. BERT has no such factorisation — scoring the sentence above took O(n) forward passes, one per masked position. The same property that makes an encoder good at reading makes it bad at generating, and no amount of engineering gets around it. BERT cannot write.

Where BERT sits

BERT (Devlin et al., 2019) did not introduce the Transformer, attention, or masking. It introduced a default: pre-train one large encoder on unlabelled text, then fine-tune it on whatever small labelled task you have. Before BERT, a new NLP task meant a new architecture and a corpus to go with it. After it, the task meant a head and a few thousand labels. The GLUE leaderboard turned over in months.

The lineage in this series is direct. Part 1 built word vectors that were static — bank had one vector whether it sat by a river or held your money. Part 2 decided which subword pieces words decompose into; BERT’s WordPiece is literally Part 2’s second algorithm. Part 3 built the encoder-decoder pair, and this article keeps the encoder, deletes the mask, and swaps the training signal from translation to reconstruction. The result is the thing word embeddings could not be: a vector per occurrence, computed from the company that particular word is keeping.

What BERT’s descendants changed was mostly the recipe, not the machine. RoBERTa dropped NSP, trained longer, and masked dynamically. ALBERT shared parameters across layers. DeBERTa disentangled content from position. ELECTRA replaced masked prediction with corruption detection — which is, almost exactly, the acceptability task above. The encoder stayed where it was.

What comes next

Part 5, GPT-style Decoders, takes the other half. The causal mask comes back, and with it the one thing this article’s encoder cannot do: generate. The question is what you gain when the price of reading only backwards is paid willingly — a gradient from every token, a sentence scored in one pass, and text that runs on past its prompt.

The complete code

The full script is on GitHub — grab it and run it:

bert_encoder.py

Run it with:

pip install numpy
python bert_encoder.py

It needs only numpy and runs in about four minutes. Everything is from scratch: bidirectional multi-head self-attention with a padding mask, LayerNorm, learned positional embeddings, the masked-LM head and the [CLS] head, the 80/10/10 corruption recipe, fine-tuning and frozen probing, and pseudo-log-likelihood scoring — all hand-differentiated and audited against central differences at startup (worst error 8.22e-10 over 330 sampled gradients). The headline insight worth pinning to the wall: bidirectionality is not a general improvement, it is a precisely located one — on the slots whose evidence lies to the left, a causal encoder ties the bidirectional one at 100%, and on the slot whose evidence lies to the right it scores 66.7%, which is exactly the base rate for guessing the majority class, against 99.8%; the pre-training that buys this also buys transfer, taking a 100-label task from 55.7% to 80.0%, but the frozen probe never passes 62%, because masked language modelling gives [CLS] nothing to do — which is what next-sentence prediction was for, and why fine-tuning, not probing, is how BERT is used; and the cheapest route of all skips the head entirely, scoring sentences straight from the masked-LM output at 90.5% with twenty labels spent on a single threshold.
