Part 1 ended with an insurance policy. fastText built every word from character 3–5-grams, and when three words that had never appeared in the corpus arrived for pricing, their n-gram vectors landed beside their singulars anyway. Morphology was the insurance — but the pieces were hand-chosen: every trigram, every 4-gram, every 5-gram, mechanically enumerated. A six-letter word generates 28 of them. Most are noise.

This article asks a sharper question:

    can the corpus decide which pieces to keep?

Three algorithms, three answers. Byte-pair encoding (Sennrich et al., 2016) starts from individual characters and greedily merges the most frequent adjacent pair — repeating until a vocabulary budget is spent. WordPiece (Schuster & Nakajima, 2012; used by BERT) runs the same loop but scores by mutual information instead of raw count: which pair is most surprising given how common each part is on its own? Unigram LM (Kudo, 2018; used by SentencePiece, T5, Llama) goes the other way entirely: start with every substring, then prune by how much each token’s removal hurts corpus likelihood, until the budget is met.

The stage is Part 1’s synthetic micro-language — the same three topics, 106 words, 165,166 tokens — so the same words whose embeddings we measured can now be measured through a different lens: not what neighbours does a word have? but what pieces should a word be built from?

A worked example: merge, score, prune, compress

BPE: greedy merging from characters

Start from individual characters plus an end-of-word marker (_). Count every adjacent pair in the corpus. Merge the most frequent. Repeat.

The first fifteen merges on Part 1’s corpus:

    merge  1:  'h'  + 'e'    ->  'he'
    merge  2:  't'  + 'he'   ->  'the'
    merge  3:  'the'+ '_'    ->  'the_'
    merge  4:  's'  + '_'    ->  's_'
    merge  5:  'r'  + '_'    ->  'r_'
    merge  6:  'a'  + 'n'    ->  'an'
    merge  7:  'e'  + '_'    ->  'e_'
    merge  8:  's'  + 't'    ->  'st'
    merge  9:  'o'  + 'r'    ->  'or'
    merge 10:  'the'+ 'i'    ->  'thei'
    merge 11:  'thei'+'r_'   ->  'their_'
    merge 12:  'n'  + '_'    ->  'n_'
    merge 13:  'd'  + '_'    ->  'd_'
    merge 14:  'r'  + 'o'    ->  'ro'
    merge 15:  'a'  + 'r'    ->  'ar'

The corpus speaks immediately. Merge 1–3 build the_ — the most common word. Merges 10–11 build their_ — the third most common. No rule tells BPE about English articles or possessives; frequency alone promotes them. By merge 200 the entire corpus vocabulary fits in 141 tokens, and the average word is 1.61 tokens long:

    king           -> king_
    queen          -> queen_
    harvests       -> harvests_
    navigates      -> navigates_

Single tokens for common words, exactly as you would hope. But the real test is the words BPE has never seen. Part 1’s three held-out plurals — never generated, zero occurrences — decompose into known pieces:

    princesses     -> prince | s | s | es_   (OOV)
    roosters       -> roost | e | rs_        (OOV)
    compasses      -> compa | s | s | es_    (OOV)

Every piece was learned from other words in the corpus. prince from prince, es_ from every word ending in -es. The merge table is the tokeniser: 200 lines that say “whenever you see X next to Y, join them.” Apply them in order to any new string and it decomposes — no dictionary lookup, no hand-written rules. The pieces the corpus chose are the pieces the model gets to see.

WordPiece: the same loop, a different score

WordPiece replaces raw frequency with a mutual-information criterion:

    score(a, b) = freq(ab) / (freq(a) * freq(b))

A pair scores high when it appears together more often than you would expect from each part’s individual frequency. On the same corpus, the first fifteen merges diverge completely:

     #   BPE                  WordPiece
    ---  ----                 ---------
     1   'h'+'e'              'q'+'u'
     2   't'+'he'             'u'+'g'
     3   'the'+'_'            'o'+'ug'
     4   's'+'_'              'l'+'oug'
     5   'r'+'_'              'p'+'loug'
     6   'a'+'n'              'u'+'l'
     7   'e'+'_'              'b'+'ul'
     8   's'+'t'              'bul'+'l'
     9   'o'+'r'              'g'+'ul'
    10   'the'+'i'            'gul'+'l'
    11   'thei'+'r_'          'm'+'u'
    12   'n'+'_'              'mu'+'d'
    13   'd'+'_'              'mud'+'d'
    14   'r'+'o'              'mudd'+'y'
    15   'a'+'r'              'o'+'u'

BPE builds the first — the most frequent word. WordPiece builds bull, gull, muddy — rare words whose letter sequences are perfectly predictable. q appears only before u in this corpus, so the pair q+u has maximum mutual information despite modest count. The rare-pair bonus makes WordPiece greedy on morphologically informative fragments: it learns whole stems and suffixes earlier because they co-occur reliably, even when their raw count is modest.

Of 200 merges, only 35 are shared. The two algorithms segment the same words differently:

    princess    BPE:       princess_
                WordPiece: princ | e | s | s | _
 
    compasses   BPE:       compa | s | s | es_
                WordPiece: compass | e | s | _

WordPiece builds compass as a single piece — the stem is informative — while BPE keeps compa and es_ separately, stitched from frequency. BERT chose WordPiece; GPT chose BPE. The difference is in which fragments get promoted, not in the promise: both decompose anything into known pieces.

Unigram LM: start big, prune by likelihood

BPE and WordPiece build up from characters. Unigram LM (the algorithm inside SentencePiece) goes the other way: start with every observed substring up to a length cap, then iteratively prune.

At each round: (1) estimate token probabilities from the current best segmentation of every training word (EM), (2) score each token by how much corpus log-likelihood would drop if it were removed, (3) drop the least painful fraction — but never single characters. Repeat until the vocabulary budget is met.

Segmentation is not merge-by-merge but global: the Viterbi algorithm (dynamic programming) finds the highest-probability tokenisation of every word under the current unigram model.

Trained on Part 1’s corpus with a target of 80 tokens:

    Top-5 tokens by probability:
      'the_'    p = 0.1296
      '_'       p = 0.0979
      's'       p = 0.0731
      'e'       p = 0.0662
      'r'       p = 0.0428

The in-vocabulary words segment cleanly:

    king       -> king_
    queen      -> queen_
    princess   -> p | rincess_
    navigates  -> n | a | vigates_

But the held-out words reveal a genuine difference in character. With 80 vocabulary slots and roughly 128 unique word forms, the Unigram model memorises the most frequent whole words and spends nothing on intermediate subword pieces — it never needs them for any training word. The OOV fallback is pure character-level:

    princesses -> p|r|i|n|c|e|s|s|e|s|_  (OOV)
    roosters   -> r|o|o|s|t|e|r|s|_      (OOV)
    compasses  -> c|o|m|p|a|s|s|e|s|_    (OOV)

This is not a bug — it is the honest consequence of the algorithm’s optimality criterion. BPE and WordPiece must pass through intermediate pieces (like prince, roost, es_) because they build bottom-up; those intermediates happen to be useful for OOV. Unigram, building top-down, never pays for pieces it does not need on the training corpus. At web scale — millions of unique words against tens of thousands of vocabulary slots — the math reverses: whole words will not fit, so the model learns genuinely useful subword pieces and OOV handling improves. The difference is one of scale, not design.

Comparing all three on OOV:

    word          BPE                WordPiece
    ----          ---                ---------
    princesses    prince s s es_     princ e s s e s _
    roosters      roost e rs_        roost e r s _
    compasses     compa s s es_      compass e s _
 
    word          Unigram (80 tokens)
    ----          -------
    princesses    p r i n c e s s e s _
    roosters      r o o s t e r s _
    compasses     c o m p a s s e s _

Three algorithms, three segmentation styles, one shared promise: no word is unsayable.

The compression curve

Every merge trades one vocabulary slot for shorter sequences:

    merges  vocab  tok/word  corpus tokens
    ------  -----  --------  -------------
         0     25      6.65        970,502
        25     48      4.68        578,521
        50     70      3.87        470,663
       100     98      2.92        349,226
       150    119      2.17        268,047
       200    133      1.61        206,161
       300    128      1.00        165,166

At zero merges every character is a token: maximum sequence length, minimum vocabulary. At 300 merges every word is a single token: minimum length, maximum vocabulary. Real tokenisers sit somewhere in between. GPT-4 uses roughly 100,000 BPE tokens; Llama uses 32,000 Unigram tokens. The choice is architectural: a larger vocabulary means shorter sequences (faster attention — which is O(n²) in length — and more context per window) but a bigger embedding matrix. BPE’s merge count is the single knob that sets this tradeoff.

At 300 merges the vocabulary shrinks from 200-merge levels (133 → 128) because late merges subsume earlier intermediate tokens that are no longer needed. The vocabulary curve is not monotone — it peaks and then contracts as whole words replace their parts.

Big-O and complexity

![[BIG-O TABLE IMAGE]]

BPE learning scans every word’s symbol sequence once per merge — O(M · N) where M is the merge count and N the number of symbol pairs in the corpus. Application (encoding a new word) replays M merges over the word’s characters: O(M · L) for a word of length L. WordPiece has the same complexity with a different scoring function. Unigram’s pruning loop is heavier — each round runs Viterbi over every training word (O(L²) per word per vocabulary candidate) — but runs offline; at inference it is a single Viterbi pass, O(L² · |V|) in the worst case, O(L²) with a trie.

In practice all three run in seconds on corpora with millions of words. Tokenisation is a preprocessing step: it runs once before training and once per input at inference, so even quadratic-in-word-length costs vanish against the model’s own O(n² · d) attention. The real cost is indirect: vocabulary size sets the embedding matrix’s row count, and sequence length sets the attention bill. The compression curve above is the complexity story — every merge shortens every sequence at the price of one more embedding row.

Where tokenisation sits

Tokenisation is the invisible first decision of every language model. GPT-2 (Radford et al., 2019) adopted BPE as its tokeniser and the field followed; BERT (Devlin et al., 2019) chose WordPiece; T5 (Raffel et al., 2020) and Llama (Touvron et al., 2023) chose SentencePiece’s Unigram. The choice shapes what the model can and cannot see: the same sentence becomes a different number of tokens under each scheme, and attention’s O(n²) cost means that difference compounds through every layer.

The connection to Part 1 is direct. BPE’s merge loop is fastText’s character n-grams with a learned filter: instead of enumerating every 3-to-5-gram, BPE keeps only the substrings the corpus promotes. The Levy–Goldberg equivalence (SGNS = shifted PMI factorisation) showed that word embeddings are implicitly compressing co-occurrence statistics; BPE compresses the words themselves, by the same principle — frequent patterns get shorter codes. Sennrich et al. borrowed the algorithm from data compression (Gage, 1994), and the analogy is exact: BPE was invented to shrink files, and it shrinks vocabularies by the same greedy logic.

The complete code

The full script is on GitHub — grab it and run it:

bpe_tokenisation.py

Run it with:

pip install numpy

It needs only numpy and runs in about thirty seconds. Everything is from scratch: BPE’s greedy merge loop, WordPiece’s mutual-information scoring, the Unigram LM with Viterbi segmentation and EM-trained probabilities, and the compression curve that shows the vocabulary-vs-length tradeoff. The headline insight worth pinning to the wall: the corpus decides the pieces — BPE promotes frequent pairs (building the_ first), WordPiece promotes surprising pairs (building bull and gull before the), and Unigram prunes from above by likelihood cost; all three decompose unseen words into known fragments, but the bottom-up methods (BPE, WordPiece) keep useful intermediate subword pieces by construction, while top-down Unigram invests its budget in whole words and falls back to characters for the unknown — an honest consequence of optimising for the training corpus, not for words it has never seen; the compression curve (6.65 tokens per word at zero merges, 1.00 at 300) is the single architectural knob that decides how large a model’s vocabulary should be, trading embedding rows for sequence length against attention’s quadratic cost.
