Every article in this track so far has compared a prediction against an answer. Part 4 recovered a masked word that was really there. Part 6 assigned one of three labels that a function computed. Even Part 5, which generated text, judged it by asking whether each sentence was grammatical.

Generation proper has no answer to compare against. There are many good continuations of a prompt, the model does not emit one sentence but a distribution over sentences, and the thing you ship is not the model — it is the model plus a rule for turning that distribution into words. Part 5 shipped the simplest such rule and deliberately stopped there:

temperature 0   always take the likeliest token
temperature 1   sample from the model as it is
temperature 2   flatten first, then sample

Top-k, nucleus sampling and beam search were left for this article. So was the question underneath them: when there is no single right answer, what exactly is being measured?

Here, uniquely, that question has an arithmetic answer.

A worked example: the decoder is a distribution

The move this article is built on

Part 1’s micro-language is enumerable. All 31,740 of its sentences can be listed, their probabilities sum to exactly one, and its entropy is 9.2538 nats. Part 5 used that to put a floor under the decoder’s loss.

Now notice what temperature, top-k and nucleus sampling actually are. Each one rewrites the next-token distribution and then samples from the rewrite. The rewrite is deterministic. So the probability that the whole strategy produces a particular sentence is just a product:

q(s) = prod_t q_trunc(s_t | s_<t)

and that product can be evaluated for every sentence in the language rather than estimated by sampling. A decoding strategy is a distribution, and in this universe we can compute it exactly.

One pre-trained decoder fixes everything that follows. It is Part 5’s, unchanged — 114,286 parameters, held-out loss 1.4706 nats per token against the language’s floor of 1.4190, with 0.9770 of its probability mass sitting on real sentences. From here the model never changes again. Only the decoding does.

What truncation actually costs

![[FRONTIER TABLE IMAGE]]

Four columns, no sampling error in any of them. Grammatical is the exact probability the output is a sentence of the language. Can say counts the sentences the strategy is capable of producing at all. Effective sentences is the exponential of its entropy. TV is total-variation distance from the truth.

Read the top-k 2 row twice. It is 99.97% grammatical and it can produce 16 of the 31,740 sentences — three tenths of one percent of the language. Its grammar is nearly perfect because there is almost nothing left for it to say.

That is the trade in its rawest form, and it does not go away at sensible settings. Nucleus 0.90 is 99.93% grammatical and has quietly made more than half the language unsayable: 14,032 sentences remain reachable out of 31,740.

The TV column makes the point that the quality column hides. Sampling the model untruncated sits 0.2880 away from the truth. Every truncation setting in the table except the gentlest moves it further away, while making the output look better. Truncation does not repair a model. It trades breadth for the appearance of competence, and the exchange rate is steep.

A consistency check, because the whole article rests on the exact computation being right: drawing 1,000 samples per strategy gives 0.9810 against the computed 0.9770 for temperature 1.0, 0.9950 against 0.9961 for top-k 10, and 0.9990 against 0.9993 for nucleus 0.90 — each inside one standard error of sampling.

Why nucleus beats top-k

The two methods are usually compared by showing people paragraphs and asking which they prefer. Here they can be compared by arithmetic:

top-k 10      gramm 0.9961 | eff sentences 2257
nucleus 0.90  gramm 0.9993 | eff sentences 5555

Nucleus wins on quality, wins on diversity, and sits less than half as far from the truth. The reason is in the mechanism. A fixed k has to be chosen for the model’s flattest prediction, because that is where cutting too hard does the damage — but the same k then applies where the next token is nearly determined, keeping nine words that should never have been candidates. A fixed probability mass adapts: two candidates where the model is confident, many where it is not.

That is Holtzman et al.’s argument for nucleus sampling, and it reproduces here as measurement rather than as preference.

Beam search finds the wrong sentence

![[BEAM TABLE IMAGE]]

Beam search is not a sampler. It searches for the sequence of highest total probability, and it succeeds — which is the problem.

A sentence sampled from this model costs 9.2793 nats. The language’s entropy is 9.2538. Beam search returns sentences at 6.1840, about three nats more probable than a typical sentence — a factor of roughly 22. The sentence it finds is not a representative draw from the distribution; it is an extreme point of it.

This is a property of distributions, not a bug in beam search. In a distribution over 31,740 sentences, nearly all the mass sits on sentences that are individually unlikely. The mode is a tiny, atypical corner. Asking for the single likeliest output and hoping for a representative one is asking for two different things.

Watch what the larger beams do, too. Beam 25 finds a shorter, blander sentence than beam 1, because every extra word multiplies in another probability below one. Length normalisation is the standard patch, and it is visibly a patch: dividing by length puts the six-word sentence back on top without any principle saying it should be there.

None of this makes beam search wrong. It makes it right for a different job — translation, where one input has roughly one correct output and probability is genuinely what you want. Part 3’s sequence-to-sequence model was exactly that job. It is wrong for open-ended generation, where the output wanted is a sample and the mode is not one.

The metric problem

Now the harder half. Suppose you want to score generated text. The prompt the king has 92 valid completions in this language, and because the language is enumerable, that is not an estimate — it is the complete set.

A real benchmark shows the scorer one of them. Sometimes four. So the metric itself can be put on trial here, which is rarely possible: pair every valid sentence with a twin broken by exactly one token, confirmed outside the language by the same oracle that defines it, and score both against references drawn from the other valid completions.

![[METRIC TABLE IMAGE]]

Against a single reference a perfect generator scores 0.4899. It is not wrong. It is differently right, and BLEU has no way to tell those apart.

The second column is the serious one. The sentence the oracle rejects scores 0.4178 and matches or beats its valid twin 55.07% of the time. On one reference, BLEU ranks broken output at or above correct output more often than not.

More references help — at sixteen, the broken sentence wins only 16.67% of the time. But sixteen references per example is not what benchmarks have, and the deficit never closes: even there, a perfect generator scores 0.7921.

Which breaks BLEU catches is the mechanism, not a mystery:

![[BREAK TABLE IMAGE]]

The possessive break is caught every single time. The topic break is caught almost never — 93.48% of the time the sentence that mixes a sea noun into a royal sentence scores at or above a valid alternative. The reason is pure n-gram bookkeeping. Changing his to her destroys a bigram the reference shares; swapping the object for a word from another topic replaces a word the reference did not contain either, so nothing is lost that was ever counted.

Here is the cleanest case. Against one fixed reference, 157 of 273 pairs score exactly the same for the valid and the broken sentence:

reference : the king commands his lord
valid     : the king commands his banner    BLEU 0.7521
broken    : the king commands his captain   BLEU 0.7521

Both differ from the reference in exactly one word, so the n-gram counts are identical and so are the scores. BLEU is not malfunctioning. It is counting overlapping word strings, which is what it has always said it does. The failure is in reading that count as a measure of whether the output is any good.

What a sample cannot see

One more reason the exact computation was worth the trouble.

![[PROXY TABLE IMAGE]]

The usual diversity proxy is the share of samples that come out distinct. Read along any row: the number falls as you draw more samples, because distinctness is a birthday problem and not a property of the decoder. At 100 samples, temperature 1.0 and nucleus 0.90 are indistinguishable — both 0.9800 — while their effective sentence counts differ by a factor of 1.6. The proxy goes blind exactly where the decision between two strategies gets made.

The effective sentence count does not move, because it is a property of the distribution rather than of the experiment. That is the whole argument for building a universe you can enumerate: the quantities that actually decide a decoding strategy — how much of the language it can still reach, how far it has moved from the truth — are not estimable from any feasible number of samples. Here they are arithmetic.

Big-O and complexity

![[BIG-O TABLE IMAGE]]

Compare the two middle rows. Sampling generates a sentence in n forward passes. Beam search costs B times that, for an output that is less representative, not more — you pay twenty-five times over to move further from a typical sample. Every truncation rule is free by comparison: a sort or a partial sort over the vocabulary, dwarfed by the forward pass that produced the logits it is sorting.

The last row is the one that does not generalise. Computing a strategy’s exact distribution costs a pass over every sentence in the language, which is possible here and impossible anywhere real — that is the price of the yardstick, and it is why the rest of the field estimates.

Which is worth stating plainly: the decoding strategy is the cheapest part of the system and one of the most consequential. It is also the part most often chosen by copying a default out of someone else’s configuration file.

Where NLG sits

BLEU is from 2002, built at IBM for machine translation, where its central assumption — that there is essentially one right answer and overlap with it measures quality — was defensible. It then spread to every text generation task, including the ones where that assumption is simply false.

Beam search arrived in neural NLP through translation too, in the sequence-to-sequence models of 2014. For a decade it was the decoding algorithm, and generated text was known to be strangely repetitive and flat without anyone quite naming why. Fan et al. introduced top-k sampling in 2018. Holtzman et al. named the problem in 2019 — neural text degeneration — and showed that the flatness came from searching for high probability at all, not from any defect in the models. Nucleus sampling is their fix, and it is still the default in most systems you will use today. The entropy argument in this article is the one Meister et al. later made formally under the name typical decoding.

The lineage in this series is direct. Part 5 built the decoder and measured its distribution against the language’s; this article leaves that distribution untouched and changes only how it is read, which turns out to matter as much as anything pre-training did. Part 3’s beam-search-shaped task — one input, one correct output — is exactly the job beam search is still right for, and exactly the job this one is not.

The bi-encoder that Part 6 ended on becomes the retrieval half, the decoder becomes the generation half, and a new failure mode appears that neither article could produce on its own: output that is fluent, confident and contradicted by the document it was supposedly reading.

The complete code

The full script is on GitHub — grab it and run it:

nlg.py

Run it with:

pip install numpy
python nlg.py

It needs only numpy and runs in about seventy-five seconds. Everything is from scratch: the causal transformer, the truncation rules, batched sampling, beam search with length normalisation, BLEU, and the exact closed-form analysis that computes each strategy’s distribution over all 31,740 sentences — all hand-differentiated and audited against central differences at startup (worst error 1.76e-09 over 104 sampled gradients), with the oracle checked against the generator it claims to describe and each truncation rule checked against its own definition. The headline insight worth pinning to the wall: a decoding strategy is a distribution, not a setting, and truncation does not improve a model but trades away what it can say — top-k 2 reaches 99.97% grammaticality by making 31,724 of 31,740 sentences impossible, nucleus 0.90 quietly removes more than half the language, and every setting that makes output look better moves it further from the truth; meanwhile the metric most used to score the result cannot distinguish a valid sentence from one broken by a single word, scoring 157 of 273 such pairs exactly the same.
