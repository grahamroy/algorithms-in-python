Part 8 put the knowledge beside the model. A retriever found a document, a generator read it, and nothing in the weights had to change to add a fact.

It also left a bruise. The fine-tuning that taught the generator to defer to documents dropped its closed-book accuracy from 0.6111 to 0.0833. Something was written over, and the article could only watch it happen.

This one asks what, exactly — and whether all of it needed to move. The fine-tune is Part 8’s, unchanged. What is new is that the weights here are small enough to look at directly:

dW = W_after - W_before

That matrix is the entire record of what fine-tuning did. In a real model it is far too large to examine. In this one you can take its SVD.

Which matters, because the most popular idea in fine-tuning rests on a claim about it.

A worked example: reading an update

LoRA’s premise, and how to check it

LoRA freezes W and learns a low-rank correction instead:

W_eff = W + (alpha / r) * A @ B

with A random, B zero, so the adapted model starts exactly where the base model stopped. Only A and B ever receive a gradient. The premise is that this is enough because dW has low intrinsic rank — that fine-tuning, despite touching every weight, really only moves the model in a handful of directions.

That premise is normally argued from results. Here it can be decomposed.

![[SPECTRUM TABLE IMAGE]]

r90% is the rank a truncated SVD needs in order to keep 90% of the update’s energy. Out of 64 available directions it runs into the twenties almost everywhere. Rank 8 — a perfectly ordinary LoRA setting — captures between 52% and 70% of what full fine-tuning actually did, the output head aside.

On the strong reading of the claim, that dW is low rank, the answer here is plainly no.

One row stands apart. The output head Wv moved furthest in relative terms, 0.4461 against 0.09 to 0.19 for the attention matrices, and 46.23% of its change sits in a single direction. That is the fine-tune learning which tokens to emit in the answer slot, and it is nearly a rank-one edit. Remember that row; it sets a trap two sections from now.

Two claims that sound like one

Here is the distinction the spectrum forces:

The update is low rank — false here. A low-rank update suffices — a different claim entirely.

They sound alike and they are not. LoRA never tries to reproduce dW. It is not compressing the full fine-tune; it is solving the same problem from scratch inside a smaller space. Whether that space is big enough is an empirical question that the SVD of someone else’s solution does not answer.

So: does it suffice?

![[PLACEMENT TABLE IMAGE]]

A warning before reading the order. The same configuration varies by up to eleven points across initialisations, because B starts at zero and where A happens to point decides which subspace the adapter gets to search. Every LoRA figure in this article is a mean over four seeds, with the spread printed beside it. The standard error on each mean is about 0.013, so differences smaller than roughly 0.04 are not differences.

With that said, where the adapters go survives the test easily. Adapting everything scores 0.5417 against attention alone at 0.4549 — and both bury the head on its own at 0.2569.

Which is the trap. The head is the matrix that moved furthest, and it is the one place where dW really is close to rank one. Adapting it alone is still the worst thing you can do. The spectrum tells you where the change happened; it does not tell you where the change had to be made. A fine-tune rewrites the output head because the layers beneath it have already done the work of representing the answer — give the adapter only the head and there is nothing underneath for it to read.

How much rank

![[RANK TABLE IMAGE]]

The column rises to rank 8 and stops. Rank 16 scores 0.5660 against rank 8’s 0.5903 on twice the parameters — a gap inside the standard error, so the honest reading is that more rank stops helping, not that it hurts.

Rank 8 reaches 98.84% of full fine-tuning on 15.45% of the parameters. Rank 2 gets 87.21% of it for 3.86%. That is the trade, and it is a good one, even though full fine-tuning stays ahead throughout.

Note what the spectrum did and did not predict. It said rank 8 would capture barely half the update — and rank 8 recovers almost all of the accuracy. Those are consistent, and the gap between them is the whole point: the adapter is not approximating the update, so the energy it would have captured was never the right thing to measure.

The learning rate, and a piece of advice that fails here

Common practice is to raise the learning rate for LoRA, on the reasoning that a factorisation starting from zero has further to travel. Measured over four seeds:

lr 5e-04:  0.5417   (sd 0.0260)
lr 2e-03:  0.5035   (sd 0.0690)

No better in the mean, and 2.7 times the spread. At this scale the larger step buys variance, not accuracy.

This is worth dwelling on because of how it nearly got written up the other way. Here are the four seeds behind those two means:

lr 5e-04 : 0.5694 0.5000 0.5417 0.5556
lr 2e-03 : 0.5972 0.4028 0.5139 0.5000

A single run at the larger step could have reported 0.5972 — matching full fine-tuning, the best result anywhere in this article — or 0.4028, which is nearly the worst. A single run is what most such comparisons are, and the first seed I drew happened to be the lucky one.

What it costs the model underneath

Part 8 could only watch closed-book accuracy fall. Here the language enumerates, so the damage is exact: held-out nats per token against the oracle’s floor of 1.4310, and KL(language ‖ model) summed over all 31,740 sentences.

![[FORGETTING TABLE IMAGE]]

Read the nats column down. The learning rate moves it from about 2.3 to about 3.9. The method moves it by 0.009.

Forgetting here is a property of how far you stepped, not of which parameters you stepped in. The folk claim is that LoRA forgets less because it writes to fewer weights. On this measurement it does not forget less — it forgets the same, and the thing that actually governs the damage is the one knob the comparison usually holds constant by accident.

The mass column disagrees, and it is printed rather than buried: LoRA keeps 0.5552 of the language’s probability against full fine-tuning’s 0.3111. The two columns answer different questions — how well the model still predicts the language, and how much of its output is still spent on it. LoRA ties on the first and wins the second.

So what does LoRA actually guarantee? Something structural, not statistical. The base weights were never written to. Detach the adapter and the original model is back, bit for bit — which the script asserts at startup. No amount of measuring forgetting can give you that, and it is the property that lets fifty fine-tunes be served from one copy of the weights.

Big-O and complexity

![[BIG-O TABLE IMAGE]]

The row people get wrong is the fourth. LoRA does not make the backward pass cheaper. To obtain the gradient for A and B you first need the gradient with respect to the effective matrix, which costs exactly what full fine-tuning costs — the factor gradients are then projections of it, dA = s·dW·Bᵀ and dB = s·Aᵀ·dW. That is why the implementation needs no new derivation, and also why LoRA is not a training-speed optimisation.

What it saves is everything that scales with the number of parameters you own: the optimiser state, which for Adam is two more copies of every trainable weight, and the storage per task. The last row is the one that built an industry — N tasks cost N adapters rather than N models.

Where this sits

Adapters are from 2019: Houlsby et al. inserted small trained bottlenecks between frozen layers and got most of fine-tuning for a few percent of the parameters. The missing piece was that their adapters changed the architecture, so they cost latency at inference.

The claim this article tests came from Aghajanyan et al. in 2020, who measured the intrinsic dimension of fine-tuning and found that models could be tuned within surprisingly small random subspaces. Hu et al. turned that into LoRA in 2021, with the decisive engineering detail: because the correction is linear, W + sAB can be multiplied out and folded into W before serving, so a merged adapter costs nothing at inference. QLoRA added quantised base weights in 2023 and made the whole thing run on one consumer GPU.

The lineage in this series is direct. Part 8 made a generator defer to a document and recorded the collateral damage. This article identifies the damage, finds that most of it follows from step size rather than method, and shows that the cheapest version of the same fine-tune recovers almost all of it. Part 6’s discipline shows up again, in a new place: do not read an ordering off a single run when the thing you are ordering has a standard deviation.

What comes next

Part 10, RLHF and DPO, changes what the gradient is made of. Everything in this track so far has trained against a target that was known — the next word, a computed label, a document’s answer. Preference learning has no target at all, only the statement that one output was better than another, and the first thing that has to be built is a loss that can be assembled out of comparisons.

The complete code

The full script is on GitHub — grab it and run it:

lora.py

Run it with:

pip install numpy
python lora.py

It needs only numpy. It is the slowest script in this series, at about twenty-two minutes, because almost every number in it is a mean over four seeds and there was no honest way to make it cheaper. Everything is from scratch: the frozen base plus W + (alpha/r)AB with B initialised to zero, the projection of the base gradient onto the two factors, the singular-value machinery for reading an update, and the exact forgetting measurement over all 31,740 sentences — all hand-differentiated and audited against central differences at startup, through the LoRA factors as well as the base head (worst error 1.20e-09 over 96 sampled gradients). The headline insight worth pinning to the wall: the update that full fine-tuning produces is not low rank — reaching 90% of it takes a rank in the twenties out of 64 — and LoRA works anyway, because it never tries to reproduce that update; rank 8 recovers 98.84% of full fine-tuning’s accuracy on 15.45% of the parameters, while which matrices you adapt matters more than how much rank you give them, and the forgetting everyone attributes to the method turns out to be a property of the learning rate instead.
