RAG is two parts bolted together, and this track has already built both. Part 6 ended on a bi-encoder, whose sentence vectors do not depend on what they are compared against and so can be computed once and cached — the only reason search over millions of documents is possible at all. Part 7 built the generator and measured what it costs to turn a distribution into words.

Put the first in front of the second and you have the whole architecture:

question -> retriever -> document -> generator -> answer

The appeal is obvious. The model no longer has to know things; it has to read them. Facts can be updated by editing a store instead of retraining a network, and the answer comes with a citation.

Then comes the question that is almost impossible to answer in a real system:

When the model answers, is it reading the document, or is it reciting what it already believed?

You cannot tell from an accuracy score, because the retrieved document and the model’s prior usually say the same thing. Here, for once, they can be made to disagree on purpose.

A worked example: a prior you can aim at

The role signature is the prior

Part 1’s micro-language gives every subject a role signature — the object the language expects after it. The oracle says exactly how strong that expectation is, and the pre-trained decoder has absorbed it completely: asked to finish the king commands his, the model names the role signature 97.22% of the time, with no document anywhere in sight.

So a knowledge base can be built to fight it. Each of the 144 facts — one per subject-and-verb pair — names an object of the same topic, so every document is a grammatical sentence and the planted ones cannot be spotted by their shape. What varies is whether the fact agrees with the role signature or contradicts it:

question    : what does the king command
role says   : knight
agreeing doc: the king commands his knight
contra doc  : the king commands his castle

Two documents, one question, one model. On the agreeing document, reading and reciting produce the same word. On the contradicting one they produce different words, and the difference between those two accuracies is grounding, with nothing else varying.

One more guard: the 144 facts are split 108 train / 36 test by subject-and-verb pair, so no test answer can be recalled from fine-tuning. The document is the only place the answer exists.

The retrieval half

![[RETRIEVAL TABLE IMAGE]]

The questions are built so that overlap alone cannot solve them: the question carries the verb’s base form (command) while the document carries the inflected one (commands).

The lexical baseline is not failing to find the document. It is failing to choose. On average only 0.08 documents outscore the right one — but 10.11 documents tie with it, and a tie is settled by whatever order the store happens to be in. Overlap sees the and the subject and nothing more. Worse, several verb stems in this vocabulary (crown, plough, anchor) are also nouns, so documents about entirely unrelated subjects join the tie.

The bi-encoder has learned that command and commands are the same relation, and that is the whole of its job. It is also the whole argument for dense retrieval.

With recall@1 at 1.0000, retrieval is solved at this scale. That is convenient rather than disappointing: every error from here on belongs to the generator, and there is no need to argue about which half is at fault.

Is it reading, or reciting?

![[CONDITIONS TABLE IMAGE]]

Three outcomes, counted separately: the model answered with the document’s object, with the role signature, or with neither.

Start with the second row. The two numbers are identical, and not by coincidence — on an agreeing fact the document says exactly what the role signature says, so a model that reads and a model that recites emit the same word. That row cannot distinguish them. It is also the row that every naturally-collected benchmark is built out of, because documents found in the world mostly agree with what a model trained on the world already believes.

Now the third row. Same generator, same question, a gold document in front of it: 1.0000 when the document agrees with the prior, 0.3056 when it does not. The document is barely being read. The agreeing score was a coincidence that looked like comprehension.

The fourth row is the failure mode promised at the end of Part 7. Handed a document about the right subject and the wrong relation, the model answers with that document’s object 16.67% of the time and falls back on the role signature 50.00% of the time. Both are wrong, both are fluent, and neither is supported by the text it was given.

Where grounding actually comes from

None of this is a property of the architecture. It is a property of what the generator was fine-tuned on. Sweeping the share of training documents that contradict the prior moves every column:

![[MIX TABLE IMAGE]]

![[GROUNDING CURVE IMAGE]]

The top row is the pipeline a careful engineer builds by accident. Every training document agrees with the model’s prior — which is simply what collected evidence looks like — and the result scores a flawless 1.0000 on agreeing facts and 0.0000 on contradicting ones, reciting the role signature every single time. It has learned that the document is decoration.

Read the last two columns together, because this is the finding worth keeping. As the training mix adds contradicting evidence, reciting the role signature falls cleanly from 1.0000 to 0.0278 — and being misled by a wrong document rises just as cleanly from 0.0000 to 0.8056. These are not two behaviours that happen to correlate. They are one behaviour under two names. A model that will let a document overrule what it knows is, by construction, a model that a bad retrieval can walk into the wrong answer.

Which means the retriever is not a component you can improve independently of the generator. It is the thing that decides which of those two failure modes you get.

The middle of the sweep explains its own untidiness. The “reads the document” column climbs unevenly, and the “neither” column says why: losing faith in the prior happens before learning to copy. At a 75% contradicting mix the model recites the role signature only 25.00% of the time but reads the document just 22.22% of the time, answering with some third word in the remaining 52.78%. There is a stage in grounding training where the model has stopped trusting what it knew and has not yet started trusting what it was handed.

And grounding is not free. Follow the closed-book column down: 0.6111 to 0.0833. The same training that teaches a model to defer to documents strips out its ability to answer without one.

Big-O and complexity

![[BIG-O TABLE IMAGE]]

Part 6 ended its complexity table with the row that made this article inevitable: scoring a query against N stored sentences costs N dot products for a bi-encoder and N full forward passes for a cross-encoder. That gap is the reason the retrieval half of every production system is a bi-encoder, and the reason anything needing real precision re-ranks a shortlist with a cross-encoder afterwards.

The row that surprises people is the generator’s. Prepending a document makes the input longer, and attention is quadratic in that length, so a 500-token passage in front of a 20-token question costs 676 times the attention of the question alone. Retrieval is cheap. Reading is not, and the bill arrives on every single query rather than once at indexing time.

Where RAG sits

The architecture is from 2020 — Lewis et al. named it, and Karpukhin et al. published Dense Passage Retrieval in the same year with the in-batch-negatives recipe used here. The pitch was exactly the one above: move knowledge out of the weights and into a store you can edit.

The caveat arrived more slowly. Longpre et al. showed in 2021 that when a retrieved passage is edited to disagree with what a model learned in pre-training, models frequently follow the memory rather than the passage — and that the rate depends on how the system was trained, not on how good the retriever is. This article is that experiment rebuilt small enough that the prior is a known quantity rather than an inference.

The lineage in this series is direct, and it closes a loop. Part 6’s bi-encoder is the retriever. Part 7’s decoder is the generator. Part 6’s artefact lesson — that a benchmark measures the dataset as much as the model, and the only way to know which is to run the ablation that removes the input — reappears here wearing different clothes. There, the hypothesis-only model exposed what the data leaked. Here, the contradicting document exposes what the evaluation never tested. Both are the same discipline: do not measure a system on examples where the right answer and the easy answer coincide.

What comes next

Part 9, Fine-tuning and LoRA, takes the other route to the same goal. RAG puts the knowledge beside the model, and fine-tuning puts it inside. Having just measured what it costs to teach a model to defer to a document, the question becomes what it costs to change the weights instead — and why almost nobody changes all of them any more.

The complete code

The full script is on GitHub — grab it and run it:

rag.py

Run it with:

pip install numpy
python rag.py

It needs only numpy and runs in about six minutes. Everything is from scratch: the bidirectional retriever with mean pooling, in-batch-negative contrastive training, the causal generator reading a [SEP]-joined document, question and answer with its loss on the answer span alone, and the knowledge base whose every fact is auditable against the oracle that defines the language — all hand-differentiated and checked against central differences at startup (worst error 1.26e-09 over 150 sampled gradients, covering both the generator head and the retriever’s path through pooling and normalisation). The headline insight worth pinning to the wall: a retrieval system fine-tuned only on documents that agree with what its generator already believed scores a perfect 1.0000 on agreeing facts and 0.0000 on contradicting ones, which means the benchmark was measuring the prior and not the pipeline; and the fix is not free, because the same training that raises grounding from 0.0000 to 0.6667 raises susceptibility to a wrong document from 0.0000 to 0.8056 — trusting documents and being fooled by them are one behaviour, and the retriever decides which one you get.
