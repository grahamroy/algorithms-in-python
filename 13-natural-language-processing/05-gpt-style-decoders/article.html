Part 4 kept the encoder and threw the decoder away. This one does the opposite, and the surprising part is how little code changes hands.

In the companion script the encoder and the decoder are the same class. Exactly two things differ:

    causal=True       one boolean on the attention mask
    (targets, sel)    what you hand the loss function

BERT hands that loss the original tokens at the 15% of positions it corrupted. GPT hands it the same tokens shifted one place to the left, at every position. Same trunk, same output head, same loss function. The whole argument between the two architectures is a flag and a slice.

And corruption turns out to be something only an encoder needs. A bidirectional model must hide a word, or every position would simply read its own answer off the input. The causal mask already does that work, so a decoder trains on the corpus untouched:

sentence: the thrones crown their queen
input:    [BOS] the thrones crown their queen [EOS] [PAD]
target:   the thrones crown their queen [EOS] . .

The dots are positions with no target: once [EOS] is predicted there is nothing left to guess.

Every position carries a target, including the one that predicts [EOS] — which is how the model learns where a sentence stops. On the 8,000-sentence corpus that works out at 6.49 targets per sequence against masking’s 1.24 — over the whole pre-training run, 5.25 times the gradient signal. Masked language modelling throws most of each forward pass away.

The stage is Part 1’s synthetic micro-language again, and it has one property this article leans on hard: it is small enough to enumerate.

A worked example: the shift, the floor, the sampler

A loss with a floor you can compute

Run the generator over every choice it could make and you get all 31,740 sentences the language can produce, with their exact probabilities, summing to 1. So its entropy is not estimated from a sample — it is computed. 9.2538 nats per sentence, 1.4190 per token.

That changes what a loss curve means. Ordinarily a loss of 1.47 is a number you can only compare to another run. Here there is a floor, and we know where it is:

![[FLOOR CURVE IMAGE]]

The decoder lands at 1.4706 nats per token against an irreducible 1.4190 — 3.6% above the floor, a perplexity of 4.352 where 4.133 is the best any model could do. Not approximately the best; the best, provably.

No masked model gets this yardstick. A causal model’s sentence probability is a factorisation — a product of next-token probabilities that sums to one over sequences — so it can be compared with the thing that produced the text. Masked prediction is not a factorisation of anything, which is why Part 4’s loss curve could only be read against another loss curve.

The comparison also works slot by slot, because the conditional entropy at each position is computable too:

![[FLOOR TABLE IMAGE]]

Read the floor column first. Three slots are deterministic — every sentence opens with the, the possessive is fixed the moment the subject is chosen, and the length is fixed by the template — and the model drives all three to within 0.0013 nats of zero. The verb is a uniform choice among four, so its floor is exactly ln 4 = 1.3863, and the model scores 1.4334.

The object is the widest gap at +0.2011, and that is Part 1’s doing: half the time the object is the subject’s signature partner, and a role signature is a harder thing to learn than a rule. Remember that row — it comes back.

One more detail worth a glance. The vocabulary contains four structural tokens the model should never emit mid-sentence, and the total probability it assigns to [PAD], [BOS] and [MASK] as a next token averages 2.20e-05. Nobody told it those were off limits. It learned which tokens are grammar and which are plumbing.

What the flood buys, and what it costs

Same trunk, same 1,500 steps, same corpus, 5.25x the targets. The obvious prediction is that the decoder learns everything faster. It does not, and the way it fails to is the interesting part.

Two yardsticks, both label-free apart from a single threshold fitted on 20 labels. The first is verb-number agreement: does the subject govern the verb? The evidence sits to the left, so both models can reach it. The second is whole-sentence acceptability, scored by whatever label-free scorer each model naturally has.

![[EFFICIENCY CURVE IMAGE]]

On the verb they are level. Both reach a perfect score at step 100, and the encoder is briefly ahead at step 75. Five times the gradient does not buy the agreement rule any sooner, because the rule is a single local fact and both objectives put it in front of the model constantly.

On whole sentences the gap is enormous. The decoder passes 95% at step 50, where the encoder is still at chance; the encoder does not get there until step 300. What the flood buys is not knowledge, it is calibration — a probability for the whole sentence rather than a good guess at one position.

Those scores also cost different amounts. The decoder’s probabilities factorise, so one forward pass gives the whole sentence. Part 4’s encoder had to blank one position at a time and add up the results: six passes for these sentences, O(n) in general.

Now the bill. The causal mask cannot be made free:

![[RECEIPT TABLE IMAGE]]

Those are not bad scores, they are base rates. The subject’s number comes out at 0.6670, which is precisely the rate of guessing singular every time. Subject topic comes out at 0.6650, and half these sentences carry an adjective that gives the topic away while the other half start bare — so the ceiling on a left-to-right guess is 0.5 + 0.5/3 = 0.6667. The decoder is not looking in the wrong place. There is no place to look, exactly as Part 4’s causal encoder found.

So pre-training buys the decoder a calibrated score and costs it everything to the right. What does that mean for an actual labelled task? Here is the same acceptability task Part 4 used, by five routes:

![[LABELS TABLE IMAGE]]

Two results that want separating. Fine-tuned, the encoder wins at every label count — 0.8200 against 0.6267 at 100 labels. That is not a quirk of this stage, it is the history: BERT owned the fine-tuning era, and a bidirectional reader with a fresh head on top really was the better way to spend a few hundred labels.

Scored with no new parameters at all, the decoder wins. Twenty labels spent fitting one threshold over its one-pass sentence score reach 0.9900 — beating four hundred labels spent fine-tuning the encoder, and beating its own fine-tuned self by a mile. The randomly-initialised head is the bottleneck, which is Part 4’s pseudo-log-likelihood result turned up: there, skipping the head got you 90.5%, and here it gets you 99.0% for a sixth of the compute.

That is the whole pre-train-and-prompt idea in miniature. The knowledge was in the model; the head was in the way.

Generation, which an encoder cannot do

An encoder scores text. A decoder writes it: sample a token, append it, run the model again. And because the language is enumerable we do not have to squint at the output and call it fluent — the oracle says whether a sentence is in the language, and if not, which rule broke.

![[TEMPERATURE TABLE IMAGE]]

Temperature 0 is the trap. Every sample is grammatical and they are all the same sentence: one distinct string in a thousand. Greedy decoding is not careful, it is stuck. Push the other way and grammar dissolves in a readable order: slot order goes first and stays the biggest failure, topic mixing second, and the agreement rules are the last thing left standing.

Nothing in the loss curve says where the useful band is. Only sampling finds it, and here it is 0.5 to 1.0.

At temperature 1.0 the samples look like this:

the fertile orchard ploughs the seed
the ancient ladies crown their banner
the salty compass sails the wave
the wooden tractors plough their fields
the bull grazes his tractor

Grammatical is the low bar, though, and sampling only estimates it. We can do better: the model assigns a probability to every one of the 31,740 sentences, so add them up and the question is answered exactly.

![[DISTRIBUTION TABLE IMAGE]]

The model spends 0.9770 of its probability on real sentences — and sampling at temperature 1.0 said 0.9780, which is the check that the sampler really is drawing from the model. The KL divergence from the language to the model is 0.2987 nats. That is the same quantity the held-out test set estimated at 0.3358; computed rather than sampled, the test set was a touch pessimistic.

The marginals are exact, so their deviations are real rather than noise — and they land where the floor table said they would. The model under-generates six-word sentences by 4.2 points and the signature partner by 2.4, and those are precisely the two slots with the widest loss gap. It learned the grammar almost exactly and the frequencies only approximately.

Two limits worth naming. The vocabulary is a hard wall: the plurals Part 1 held out — princesses, roosters, compasses — are not tokens, so this model cannot utter them however well it understands plurals. That is the problem Part 2’s subword pieces exist to solve.

And generation is sequential where training was parallel. One training pass scored every position of a sentence at once; writing one takes a pass per token, each re-reading the whole prefix — 21 position-reads for a sentence that training handled in one pass over 6. Caching the keys and values is the standard repair.

Prompted, the same machine continues instead of inventing:

the noble -> the noble castle commands the queen
the salty captains -> the salty captains anchor their tide
the muddy hen -> the muddy hen feeds her seeds
the kings -> the kings command their queen

Note the agreement it supplies unprompted: a plural subject takes a plural verb and their, and hen takes her. Nobody labelled that. It is simply what predicting the next token requires.

Big-O and complexity

![[BIG-O TABLE IMAGE]]

The forward pass is the same arithmetic as Part 4’s encoder — O(n² · d) for attention, O(n · d²) for the feed-forward block — because the causal mask overwrites scores that were computed either way. Masking is free.

What differs is everything around it. Signal per pass is O(n) rather than O(0.15 · n), which is the 5.25x measured above. Scoring inverts Part 4’s cost: one forward pass instead of O(n) masked ones.

And generation inverts the inversion. Training is parallel: one pass handles every position of a sequence at once, because the targets are all known in advance. Inference is sequential: n passes, each one depending on the token the last one produced. That asymmetry is the central engineering fact of modern language models, and every trick in a serving stack — KV caches, speculative decoding, batching — exists to soften it.

Where GPT sits

GPT (Radford et al., 2018) did not introduce the transformer, the causal mask, or next-token training. Part 5 of the Deep Learning track built all three. What the GPT line introduced was a bet: that one decoder, trained on enough text, would not need a new head for each new task.

GPT-1 still fine-tuned, and on that ground BERT beat it within months — which is exactly the column that wins in the labels table above. GPT-2 (2019) dropped the fine-tuning and asked for the task in the prompt. GPT-3 (2020) made that the whole interface. The architecture barely moved across the three; the bet was about what you do with the thing afterwards, and the thing that made the bet payable was the property measured here — a decoder’s output is a calibrated distribution over continuations, so a task can be posed as what comes next rather than as a new classifier.

The lineage in this series is direct. Part 1 built static word vectors. Part 2 decided what the tokens are, and set the wall this model cannot speak past. Part 3 built the encoder-decoder pair and named its halves. Part 4 took the encoder, deleted the mask, and reconstructed. This article takes the decoder, keeps the mask, and predicts — and gets, in exchange for a blindness to its right, a machine that can be scored against the truth and can write.

What comes next

Part 6, Natural Language Inference, changes the shape of the question. Everything so far has judged one sentence at a time: is it likely, is it well formed, what comes next. NLI asks what one sentence tells you about another — does the second follow from the first, contradict it, or neither? It is the task that made sentence pairs the unit of work, and it is where the encoder and the decoder meet again.

The complete code

The full script is on GitHub — grab it and run it:

gpt_decoder.py

Run it with:

pip install numpy
python gpt_decoder.py

It needs only numpy and runs in about three and a half minutes. Everything is from scratch: multi-head causal self-attention with a padding mask, LayerNorm, learned positional embeddings, the shared output head driving both the next-token and the masked objective, temperature sampling, and the exact enumeration of the language — all hand-differentiated and audited against central differences at startup (worst error 1.77e-09 over 550 sampled gradients, with the enumeration itself checked against the generator on 6,000 strings). The headline insight worth pinning to the wall: a causal model’s loss is a calibrated quantity in a way a masked model’s never is — this decoder lands 3.6% above the computed entropy of its own universe and spends 97.70% of its probability on real sentences, both exact rather than estimated; the five times more gradient it gets does not buy the agreement rule any sooner than masking does, it buys a sentence-level score, which is why the decoder passes 95% at step 50 where the encoder needs 300; and the route to a labelled task matters more than the objective, because fine-tuned the encoder wins at every label count, while scored with no new parameters at all, twenty labels spent on a single threshold beat four hundred spent fine-tuning the encoder.
