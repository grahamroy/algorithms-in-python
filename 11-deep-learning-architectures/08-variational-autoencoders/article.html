Part 7 generated by playing a game. No loss was ever written down for the forger; the score orbited instead of descending; victory had to be read off a detective reduced to coin-flipping. It worked — and every strange thing about it traced back to the missing loss function.

The variational autoencoder (Kingma & Welling, 2014) is the opposite temperament. It also generates from noise, but it writes its loss down — and everything strange and everything lovely about the VAE traces back to what that loss charges for.

The machine has two halves. An encoder maps each data point x not to a code but to a distribution over codes — a mean and a variance, z = mu(x) + sigma(x) * eps. A decoder maps the sampled code back to data space. And one objective — the ELBO — bills both halves at once:

loss  =  E[ ||x - dec(z)||^2 / (2*sigma_x^2) ]        reconstruction
       + beta * KL( q(z|x)  ||  N(0, I) )             the code's bill

The first term is ordinary: reconstruct what you were given. The second is the interesting one. It is a compression bill — the KL divergence prices, in nats, every bit of information the encoder smuggles through the latent bottleneck. Encode nothing and reconstruction is hopeless; encode everything and the bill explodes. The optimum is a negotiated settlement, and because the prior N(0, I) is part of the contract, the same decoder that reconstructs will also generate: feed it fresh Gaussian noise and it draws new data.

One trick makes the whole thing trainable: the gradient has to pass through “sample z”. Written naively, sampling blocks backpropagation. Written as z = mu + sigma * eps — the reparameterisation trick — the randomness steps out of the gradient’s path, and the loss differentiates exactly through the sampling step. The companion script’s hand-derived backward pass does precisely that, audited at startup (5.18e-07 against central differences).

The stage: Part 7’s, on purpose

The two spirals, the ring of eight Gaussians, the quality and coverage yardsticks anchored on real data — all identical to Part 7, and the script retrains Part 7’s exact GAN live (same seeds, same budget), so the head-to-head between the game and the honest loss is measured, not remembered.

A worked example: the trade, the dial, the ledger

The honest loss, and the trade

     step   elbo loss   quality   coverage
     1000       4.657     92.0%     100.0%
     2000       4.450     87.0%     100.0%   DOWN
     3000       4.307     88.2%     100.0%   DOWN
     4000       4.291     88.1%     100.0%   DOWN
     5000       4.278     89.3%      96.7%   DOWN
     6000       4.241     89.5%      98.3%   DOWN

Five DOWNs out of five. After Part 7 — where quality fell six points between checkpoints and nothing was wrong — this is the luxury the ELBO buys: one number, evaluated on held-out data, that goes down while training goes right. Then the head-to-head, with a second, harsher quality threshold:

                  q@0.10   q@0.05   coverage
    VAE             89.5%    51.3%     98.3%
    GAN (Part 7)    98.7%    58.0%     81.7%
    real data       99.5%    77.3%     93.3%

The GAN is sharper — it wins both quality thresholds. The VAE covers — 98.3% of the spiral’s arc against the GAN’s 81.7%, including the faint tails the GAN kept losing and re-finding. This is the deepest split in generative modelling, measured on one stage: the GAN’s objective is mode-seeking (better to draw some of the data perfectly than all of it loosely), the VAE’s is mode-covering (the reconstruction term charges for every real point you fail to explain). The ring makes it blunt: across three seeds the VAE keeps 8/8 modes every time — where Part 7’s slow-detective GAN kept 3/8 — but its on-mode mass runs 39–59% against the healthy GAN’s 81%, the rest smeared between the Gaussians. The VAE never loses a mode; it pays in focus. The GAN never loses focus; it pays in modes.

The beta dial

Scale the KL bill by beta and one knob sweeps the machine through three regimes — same architecture, same budget per row:

     beta   recon-mse      KL     q@0.10   q@0.05   coverage
     0.00   0.00009    12.860    86.5%    48.1%     76.7%
     0.25   0.00034     4.640    85.4%    48.8%    100.0%
     1.00   0.00203     3.173    89.5%    51.3%     98.3%
     4.00   0.01391     1.853    96.7%    54.6%     98.3%
    16.00   0.11754     0.594    99.8%    57.9%     91.7%
    64.00   0.55750     0.001    38.2%     8.2%     11.7%

At beta 0 information is free and the model is a plain autoencoder — Section 4 met it — with near-perfect reconstruction and a latent space the prior has never heard of. At beta 16 comes this article’s honest surprise: the KL squeeze regularises the decoder into the best generator on this stage — 99.8% quality beats the GAN’s 98.7%, at matching sharpness (57.9% vs 58.0% at the harsh threshold) — while reconstruction quietly dies, its error 58 times the balanced setting’s. And beta 64 is posterior collapse, the VAE’s signature failure: KL 0.001 means the latent carries nothing, every code decodes to the same mumbled average, coverage 11.7%. Part 7’s forger froze because its gradient vanished; this encoder goes silent because the price of speaking was set too high. Two architectures, two failure modes, one shape: the part of the model that should be learning is starved by the part that sets its incentives.

The dimension ledger, and the reparameterisation receipt

The KL bill is itemised per latent dimension, and that makes it a diagnostic you get for free. Give the model sixteen latent dimensions for two-dimensional data:

    per-dim KL (sorted): 1.67 1.52 0.00 0.00 ... 0.00 0.00
    dimensions alive: 2/16   quality  89.3%   coverage 100.0%

Two dimensions carry ~1.6 nats each; fourteen come back at exactly 0.00. Nobody told the model its data was two-dimensional — the loss counted. The ELBO refuses to pay for capacity it does not need, and reading the ledger tells you what your latent space actually uses.

Last, the trick underneath it all, measured. The reparameterised gradient is exact and pathwise; the classical alternative, REINFORCE, scores the sample after the fact. Same half-trained model, 2,000 single-sample estimates of the same gradient:

    grad variance, reparameterised : 33.6
    grad variance, REINFORCE       : 210.9
    same expectation, 6x the noise

And the folklore check, honestly: at this toy scale a VAE trained end-to-end with REINFORCE encoder gradients still works — final eval ELBO 4.330 against reparameterisation’s 4.241, quality 87.4% against 89.5%. Batch averaging and a two-dimensional latent absorb a 6x noise penalty. The gap compounds with latent dimension and shrinks with batch size — at image scale it is decisive, which is why reparameterisation, not REINFORCE, is on every page of the generative-model literature. A trick’s necessity can be a matter of scale; its advantage is measurable at any.

Big-O and complexity

![[BIG-O TABLE IMAGE]]

Sampling is one decoder pass — O(L · h²), the same one-pass economics that kept the GAN alive, and one network cheaper at that: no detective was ever needed. A training step is one encoder pass, one decoder pass, and a KL term that costs a trivial O(b · d_z) because both distributions are diagonal Gaussians — the closed form is the reason everyone uses them. The line the GAN could not offer: evaluation is a number the model itself produces. The ELBO is a bound on log-likelihood, comparable across runs and architectures, cheap on held-out data — where Part 7 had to build external yardsticks because its own loss could not be trusted. The bill for all this honesty is paid in sharpness: a Gaussian likelihood averages over everything it cannot explain, and the average of many plausible details is blur.

Where the honest losses went

The ELBO’s descendants run in two directions. Turn the beta dial up a little and latent dimensions specialise — beta-VAE made that the standard recipe for disentangled representations, one generative factor per surviving dimension, this article’s ledger demo grown up. Swap the Gaussian code for a codebook of discrete symbols and you get VQ-VAE, whose token grids became the language image and audio generators speak — DALL-E 1 generated by predicting VQ codes. Hierarchies of latents (NVAE, very deep VAEs) closed much of the sharpness gap. And the quiet victory: latent diffusion. Stable Diffusion runs its denoising not on pixels but inside the latent space of a trained autoencoder — the “latent” in the name is this article’s machinery, employed as the compressed stage on which Part 9’s models perform. Meanwhile the honest loss earns its keep outside generation entirely: an ELBO on held-out data flags anomalies, compresses signals, and lets chemists do gradient search in molecule space — places where “a number you can trust” beats “a sample you cannot distinguish”.

What comes next

Part 9, Diffusion Models, looks at what happens when the encoder stops being learned at all: fix it to be gradual noising — a thousand tiny steps from data to static — and train only the decoder to walk each step backwards. The result is, quite literally, a stack of VAEs with the encoder given away for free, trained on the same ELBO logic as this article — and it bought state-of-the-art sharpness at the price Part 7’s table warned about: hundreds of sequential passes for one sample.
