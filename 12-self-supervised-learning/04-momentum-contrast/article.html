Part 1 established the economics of the crowd: in contrastive learning the negatives are the signal, they all ride inside the batch, and the similarity matrix bills you O(B²) for their company. SimCLR’s answer was brute force — batches in the thousands. MoCo (He et al., 2020 — Momentum Contrast) asks the question an accountant would: must the crowd actually live in the batch?

Its answer is a queue. Keep the last K key embeddings in a running ledger; each step, score the current queries against one positive key and the entire ledger, push the new keys in, pop the oldest out:

    query q  vs  [ its key k+ | the last 1,024 banked keys ]
    push this step's keys; pop the oldest    (a FIFO queue)

A batch of eight can face a crowd of a thousand. But the naive version of this idea fails completely, and the reason is the actual invention. The ledger spans thousands of steps, so its entries were written by old versions of the encoder — and if the encoder drifts quickly, today’s queries are being compared against coordinates from yesterday’s map. MoCo’s fix is Part 3’s trick pointed backwards: encode the keys with a slow EMA twin of the query encoder. DINO’s teacher made the targets stable; MoCo’s key encoder makes the ledger stable. Gradients flow through the query side only; the twin is one weight-lerp per step; the audit at startup checks the hand backprop through the queue softmax (4.02e-10 against central differences).

The stage is shape world one last time — and, closing the section, the companion script retrains all four of its methods live for one final family table.

A worked example: the ledger, the law, the audit, the family

A crowd that does not live in the batch

    MoCo, batch 32, queue 1024 :  96.0% /  99.6%
    MoCo, batch  8, queue 1024 :  96.0% /  99.7%

(Part 1’s NT-Xent at batch 256 scored 95.1% / 99.7%.) A batch of eight walks through training with a crowd of a thousand at its back, and matches the batch-256 baseline on both probes. The crowd size and the batch size have been fully decoupled — the whole point of the design — at the cost of a queue and a second set of weights that no gradient ever touches.

The consistency law

Same budget everywhere; the only knob is the key encoder’s momentum:

    key momentum    probe15   probe-all
       0.000        31.8%     32.9%
       0.900        49.1%     51.4%
       0.990        75.1%     89.7%
       0.999        84.4%     95.4%

Momentum 0 — keys written by the live network — is chance. Not weak: dead. This is the measurement that explains why MoCo is a paper and not a footnote. The queue itself is a data structure Section 00 covered in an afternoon; a queue whose thousand entries remain mutually comparable while the network that wrote them keeps moving — that requires the slowest twin in this section, and the dose–response is monotone all the way up. Consistency, not capacity, is what the momentum buys. (The MoCo paper’s own ablation found exactly this cliff at scale; here it reproduces in 256 lines of NumPy.)

The honest audit: does the queue pay here?

    queue     probe15   probe-all
       16       85.6%     95.3%
      256       85.5%     96.0%
     4096       85.7%     95.8%

Flat. Sixteen negatives do what four thousand do, because three shape classes saturate a crowd early. And the control experiment seals it — plain NT-Xent at batch 8, fourteen in-batch negatives, no queue, no twin, given the same long budget:

    NT-Xent, batch 8, 32k steps:  95.9% /  99.9%

It matches MoCo. On shape world the queue buys nothing — and that is the finding, stated honestly rather than staged: MoCo solves a scale problem. Its machinery pays off where useful crowds number in the tens of thousands and no device holds the batch — at ImageNet resolution with ResNet encoders, which is where its results were earned. This is the third time this section has caught famous machinery not transferring to a small stage (MAE’s 75% mask ratio, reparameterisation’s necessity, now the queue), and the lesson compounds: measure before you inherit machinery.

The family, closed

All four methods of the section, canonical configurations from their own articles, retrained live on the same 4,000 unlabelled images:

                          probe15   probe-all   yardstick
    SimCLR / NT-Xent (P1)   95.1%     99.7%    the batch beside you
    MAE (P2)                49.2%     67.6%    the data itself
    DINO (P3)               94.5%     99.9%    a slow twin's targets
    MoCo (P4)               96.0%     99.6%    a ledger, kept by a twin

Every self-supervised method in this section is one answer to the same question: when two views of the world should agree, where does the yardstick for “agree” come from? SimCLR buys a fresh crowd every batch. MoCo banks the crowd and keeps the bank honest with a slow twin. DINO drops the crowd entirely and chases the twin itself. MAE never compares to anyone — the hidden three quarters of the image is yardstick enough (and its low probe row is not a defect but a different contract: Part 2 measured it winning after fine-tuning). Four pretexts, one 16×16 stage, not a label in sight.

Big-O and complexity

![[BIG-O TABLE IMAGE]]

MoCo’s ledger line replaces SimCLR’s quadratic one: scoring against the queue costs O(B · K · d) — linear in batch, with the crowd size K a free dial rather than a memory hostage. The key encoder adds one gradient-free forward pass and an O(P) weight-lerp; the queue itself is O(K · d) floats of memory — a few megabytes where SimCLR’s crowd wanted a data-parallel cluster. That arithmetic is why MoCo ran on one GPU in 2020 while SimCLR wanted a TPU pod, and why the queue idea survived into every retrieval system that banks embeddings. The economics of this whole section then land in one contrast: pretraining is the expensive, label-free purchase — by crowd, ledger, twin or blank — and every downstream task thereafter is a probe or a light fine-tune.

Where the ledgers went

MoCo v2 absorbed SimCLR’s tricks (projection head, stronger augmentations); v3 dropped the queue for ViT encoders — by then, batches had caught up with crowds — but kept the momentum twin, which proved the more durable half of the invention: today EMA twins stabilise DINO and its descendants, data2vec, semi-supervised teachers and RL targets alike. The ledger half lives on elsewhere: banked-embedding negatives are standard in retrieval and recommendation training (Section 07’s two-tower models mine hard negatives from exactly such stores), and cross-batch memory is routine in metric learning. And the section’s four answers now blend freely at the frontier — DINOv2 trains agreement and masking; contrastive towers sit beside masked backbones in every foundation-model stack. The question each method answered — where does the yardstick come from? — has stopped being a rivalry and become a menu.
