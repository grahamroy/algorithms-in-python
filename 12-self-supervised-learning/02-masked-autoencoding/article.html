Part 1 manufactured supervision by ignoring: declare which differences do not matter — the augmentation menu — and demand agreement across them. This article builds it the opposite way: by reconstructing. Hide most of the input; demand the model predict what is missing. For text, that recipe is BERT (Devlin et al., 2019) — mask words, predict words — and it trained the encoders that started the modern NLP era. For images it is the masked autoencoder, MAE (He et al., 2022): blank three quarters of the patches and regress the missing pixels.

    input:  image with 12 of 16 patches blanked (75%)
    task :  predict the hidden pixels
    loss :  MSE, scored ONLY on what was hidden

No labels, no augmentation menu, no negatives, no crowd. The supervision is the data’s own redundancy: to fill in a hidden patch of a cross you must know it is a cross, where it sits, how bright it is. An encoder squeezes the visible quarter into a 32-number code; a decoder rebuilds the hidden three quarters from that code; whatever survives the squeeze is the representation.

The stage is Part 1’s shape world, on purpose, and the companion script retrains Part 1’s contrastive encoder live — so the section’s two philosophies, learn by ignoring and learn by reconstructing, meet on one measured table. The masked-reconstruction backward pass is hand-derived and audited at startup (2.11e-10 against central differences).

A worked example: the probes, the flip, the keeping

Hide three quarters, and what the probes say

    masked-pixel reconstruction mse: 0.0467  (pixel noise^2 = 0.0100)
 
                       probe, 15 labels   probe, all 4,000
    MAE                  49.2%             67.6%
    contrastive          95.1%             99.7%

The reconstruction works — the model fills in hidden strokes it has never seen. And the linear probes call the representation a failure anyway: 49.2% against 95.1% on fifteen labels, thirty points behind even with every label in the pile. Reconstruction features are not linearly organised by class. If this article stopped here, the verdict would be: use Part 1.

The fine-tune flip

It doesn’t stop here. Unfreeze everything and fine-tune both encoders on the same fifteen labels, with Part 1’s supervised-from-scratch baseline alongside:

                       frozen probe   fine-tuned
    MAE                   49.2%         87.5%
    contrastive           95.1%         91.1%
    from scratch            --          84.9%

Read the two columns as a chiasmus, because the rows swap places. Fine-tuning transforms the masked autoencoder — 49.2% to 87.5%, past the from-scratch baseline: the class information was in there all along, just not linearly arranged, and fifteen labels sufficed to unlock it. Fine-tuning hurts the contrastive encoder — 95.1% to 91.1%: its frozen features already beat anything fifteen labels can teach, and gradient steps on so few examples only overfit. Agreement features are ready to wear; reconstruction features are raw material. The same split holds nine orders of magnitude up: MAE backbones are built to be fine-tuned, CLIP-style towers get frozen and probed — and knowing which regime your downstream budget lives in is precisely how you choose between these two pretexts.

The reconstructor keeps everything

Why are the probes so different? Shift each test image and measure how far its embedding moves, as a fraction of the typical distance between two different images:

    contrastive: shift moves the embedding 0.35x a typical distance
    MAE        : shift moves the embedding 0.83x a typical distance

The contrastive embedding barely notices the shift. The MAE embedding moves almost as far as jumping to a different image — and a linear probe reads which half of the canvas the shape sits in from the MAE embedding at 94.2%. Nothing surprising is happening: reconstruction needs position, so position is kept. Invariance was never asked for, so it never appears. The contrastive encoder is invariant — it chose what to forget; the masked autoencoder is equivariant — it is not allowed to forget anything, because any detail it drops is a detail it cannot redraw. That single design difference is the entire probe gap, and the entire fine-tune flip.

And an honest footnote. MAE’s most quoted number is its mask ratio — 75%, chosen because natural images are so redundant that easier ratios make the task trivial. On shape world:

    mask ratio   recon-mse   probe15   probe-all
       25%       0.0226      48.3%     68.7%
       50%       0.0296      48.7%     68.8%
       75%       0.0467      49.2%     67.6%
       90%       0.0609      49.6%     72.0%

Reconstruction gets three times harder from 25% to 90%; the probes barely move. Shape world is so regular that even a quarter of the pixels gives the whole game away — the 75% law lives in the statistics of natural images, and it does not automatically ride along to other data. Measure before you inherit a hyperparameter.

Big-O and complexity

![[BIG-O TABLE IMAGE]]

Masked autoencoding’s bill is refreshingly linear: one encoder pass, one decoder pass, O(B · L · h²) per step, and no O(B²) similarity matrix anywhere — the crowd of negatives that dominated Part 1’s budget simply is not on the invoice. At transformer scale the accounting gets better still: a ViT-based MAE encoder processes only the visible 25% of tokens, quartering the cost of its most expensive component — one reason MAE pretraining is cheap enough to be routine. The decoder is scaffolding, discarded after pretraining, exactly like Part 7’s detective. The bill this pretext does pay sits downstream: where a contrastive tower stops at a probe, a masked backbone usually wants full fine-tuning — cheap per task, but not free, and the right column of DEMO 2 is the reason you pay it.

Where the blanks went

Masked prediction is arguably the most consequential training idea of the era, and it did not start with images. BERT’s masked words trained the encoder half of NLP; GPT’s variant — mask nothing, predict the next token — trained the rest, and Section 13 will treat both properly. MAE brought the recipe to vision with the 75% twist and made ViT pretraining cheap; audio (HuBERT, wav2vec’s masked steps), video (VideoMAE), protein sequences (ESM) and time series followed, because the recipe asks nothing of the data except redundancy. The division of labour this article measured — reconstruction backbones for fine-tuning, agreement towers for frozen probes — is visible across the industry: the encoders behind search and recommendation are contrastive; the backbones behind detection, segmentation and medical imaging are masked pretrained then fine-tuned. And the two philosophies merged: DINOv2, today’s default vision backbone, trains agreement and masked prediction together — Part 3 meets Part 2 in one loss, which is where this section is heading.

What comes next

Part 3, Self-Distillation (DINO), returns to Part 1’s agreement objective and removes the crowd: no negatives, no contrast — just a student network chasing its own slowly-moving teacher. Part 1 measured the 158× collapse that should cause; DINO’s centring-and-sharpening trick is the reason it doesn’t, and shape world will host the whole tightrope walk.

The complete code

The full script is on GitHub — grab it and run it:

mae.py

Run it with:

pip install numpy
python mae.py

It needs only numpy and runs in about five minutes. Everything is from scratch: the patch masking, encoder, decoder, the masked-pixel loss with hand-derived audited gradients, a live retrain of Part 1’s contrastive encoder, linear probes, fine-tuning for all three contenders, and the shift-sensitivity measurement. The headline insight worth pinning to the wall: hide three quarters of the input and the model must learn what things are in order to redraw them — but what it learns is arranged for redrawing, not for reading: the linear probe scores 49.2% against the contrastive encoder’s 95.1% on the same fifteen labels, then fine-tuning flips the table — MAE leaps to 87.5%, past the 84.9% from-scratch baseline, while the contrastive encoder falls to 91.1% — because the reconstructor keeps everything (its embedding moves 0.83× an image-to-image distance under a mere shift, and position reads out at 94.2%) while the agreement encoder kept only what its menu declared essential: reconstruction features are raw material, agreement features are ready to wear, and the fine-tuning budget downstream is what should pick between them.
